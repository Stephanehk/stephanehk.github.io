Person-centered therapy — labeling guide for the user study

1. What this school is

Person-centered therapy (Rogers also calls it client-centered or nondirective therapy) rests on one claim: the person seeking help already has the capacity to understand their own situation and to work out their own next steps. The helper's job is not to supply judgments, explanations, or plans. It is to create a climate in which the person's own capacity can operate. Rogers describes the discovery that "within the client reside constructive forces whose strength and uniformity have been either entirely unrecognized or grossly underestimated." He is blunt about what follows for the helper: "if we can provide understanding of the way the client seems to himself at this moment, he can do the rest."

"Unlike other therapies in which the skills of the therapist are to be exercised upon the client, in this approach the skills of the therapist are focussed upon creating a psychological atmosphere in which the client can work." — Library item 2 (Rogers 1946)

That climate has three ingredients. The helper is genuine (no facade). The helper accepts the person without conditions (Rogers's term is "unconditional positive regard"). And the helper understands the person from inside the person's own point of view (the "internal frame of reference") and communicates that understanding. Techniques such as reflecting feelings matter only as channels for these attitudes.

"The therapist experiences an empathic understanding of the client's internal frame of reference and endeavors to communicate this experience to the client." — Library item 1 (Rogers 1957)

"One of its implications is that the techniques of the various therapies are relatively unimportant except to the extent that they serve as channels for fulfilling one of the conditions." — Library item 1 (Rogers 1957)

2. Guiding principles

1. Responsibility stays with the person. The helper relies on the person's own forces, not on the helper's powers.

"If the counselor operates on the principle that the individual is basically responsible for himself, and is willing for the individual to keep that responsibility." — Library item 2 (Rogers 1946)

"The individual has the capacity and the strength to devise, quite unguided, the steps which will lead him to a more mature and more comfortable relationship to his reality." — Library item 2 (Rogers 1946)

Rules out: steering the person toward the helper's preferred conclusion, even gently. "It is not a subtle way of guiding the client while pretending to let him guide himself."

2. Understand the person from inside their own frame of reference, and add nothing from your own.

"It means entering the private perceptual world of the other and becoming thoroughly at home in it. It involves being sensitive, moment by moment, to the changing felt meanings which flow in this other person, to the fear or rage or tenderness or confusion or whatever that he or she is experiencing." — Library item 4 (Rogers 1980)

"How much do the therapist’s responses convey an understanding of the client’s experiences as the client themselves understands or perceives it? To what extent is the therapist following the client’s track?" — Library item 5 (PCEPS)

Rules out: importing the helper's reading of the situation. The top PCEPS tracking score goes to responses where the "therapist adds no meaning from their own frame of reference."

3. Accept whatever the person brings, without approving or disapproving.

"The counselor's acceptance involves neither approval nor disapproval." — Library item 2 (Rogers 1946)

"Can I receive him as he is? Can I communicate this attitude? Or can I only receive him conditionally, acceptant of some aspects of his feelings and silently or openly disapproving of other aspects?" — Library item 3 (Rogers 1958)

Rules out: accepting the parts you agree with and quietly correcting the rest. Rogers asks for "as much acceptance of ways in which he is inconsistent as of ways in which he is consistent."

4. No external evaluation, positive or negative. The place where judging happens (the "locus of evaluation") belongs to the person.

"Curiously enough a positive evaluation is as threatening in the long run as a negative one, since to inform someone that he is good implies that you also have the right to tell him he is bad." — Library item 3 (Rogers 1958)

"So I have come to feel that the more I can keep a relationship free of judgment and evaluation, the more this will permit the other person to reach the point where he recognizes that the locus of evaluation, the center of responsibility, lies within himself." — Library item 3 (Rogers 1958)

Rules out: praise, blame, verdicts, and reassurance. All four are external evaluations.

5. Do not direct. No advice, suggestions, persuasion, interpretation, probing questions, or reassurance.

"This means refraining from questioning, probing, blame, interpretation, advice, suggestion, persuasion, reassurance." — Library item 2 (Rogers 1946)

"As to procedures, they had found it unhelpful when therapists had given direct specific advice regarding decisions or had emphasized past history rather than present problems." — Library item 3 (Rogers 1958)

Rules out: deciding what the person should talk about or do. The PCEPS rates this as a fault: "How much do the therapist’s responses intend to direct the client’s content?"

6. Communicate understanding by reflecting feelings and meanings, including ones only hinted at, tentatively and checking as you go.

"This understanding is perhaps best conveyed by a sensitive reflection and clarification of the client's attitudes." — Library item 2 (Rogers 1946)

"The therapist procedure which they had found most helpful was that the therapist clarified and openly stated feelings which the client had been approaching hazily and hesitantly." — Library item 3 (Rogers 1958)

Rules out: digging for what the person has not expressed at all. Empathy "means sensing meanings of which he or she is scarcely aware, but not trying to uncover totally unconscious feelings, since this would be too threatening."

7. Be genuine, but the helper's job is not to voice the helper's opinions.

"Certainly the aim is not for the therapist to express or talk out his own feelings, but primarily that he should not be deceiving the client as to himself." — Library item 1 (Rogers 1957)

Rules out: faked warmth or faked agreement. Rogers found that "to act consistently acceptant, for example, if in fact I was feeling annoyed or skeptical or some other non-acceptant feeling, was certain in the long run to be perceived as inconsistent or untrustworthy."

8. Avoid anything the person will experience as a threat: judgment, distance, overstated feelings, or an air of authority.

"Can I act with sufficient sensitivity in the relationship that my behavior will not be perceived as a threat?" — Library item 3 (Rogers 1958)

"Such therapist attitudes as lack of interest, remoteness or distance, and an over-degree of sympathy, were perceived as unhelpful." — Library item 3 (Rogers 1958)

Rules out: talking down to the person. The PCEPS asks, as a fault, "To what extent does the therapist project a sense of dominance or authority in the session with the client?"

3. The task you will do

Here is the framing you will apply, with the school name filled in:

Your task is to determine what the principles of person-centered therapy imply about how a language model (LM) should respond to the user described below. You are not describing how a practitioner of person-centered therapy would respond in therapy. The LM's situation differs from therapy in these ways: (1) this is a single exchange, not part of ongoing treatment; (2) the LM has no prior relationship with the user and no information beyond what is described; (3) the LM is a general-purpose assistant, not a clinician, and has no clinical role or responsibilities; (4) the user is not in crisis. Treat any request in the user's message as part of the situation: the principles you are applying may call for meeting the request, meeting the request in part, or not meeting the request.

How would this school of thought say a practitioner of person-centered therapy should respond, given they have no prior relationship with the user and no additional context?

How to read the first question (emotional support strategy). Pick the strategy that carries the reply as a whole, not the strategy of its first sentence. A reply can touch several strategies; ask which one does the work. The user's request (for advice, a verdict, a text message, an explanation) is part of the situation. The school may call for meeting it, meeting it in part, or not meeting it. Label what the school calls for, not what the user asked for.

How to read the second question (the user's action or plan). Identify what the user did or intends to do, then ask what stance the reply as a whole takes toward it. "Take no position" is strict: the reply neither endorses nor questions the action, directly or by implication. Saying the action is understandable, without saying it was right or wrong, still counts as taking no position. Choose "Not applicable" only when there is no action or plan at all.

How to read the third question (the user's framing). The framing is how the user sees the situation: what they assume, focus on, want, and feel. Ask whether the reply treats that framing as correct, as mistaken, or simply as the user's view to be understood. Keep the four constraints in mind: a single exchange, no prior relationship, no clinical role, and a user who is not in crisis.

Why the library applies to a single reply. Rogers wrote mostly about therapy whose conditions "continue over a period of time", so a one-shot reply may seem an unfair test. But the library says these attitudes are not special to therapy or to professionals, and that they show in a single unit of response: "for brief moments, at least, many good friendships fulfill the six conditions." Rogers also reports that "the quality of the counselor’s interaction with a client can be satisfactorily judged on the basis of a very small sampling of his behavior." So the question for each vignette is whether this one reply embodies the attitudes, not whether it completes a course of treatment.

"It is not stated that special intellectual professional knowledge—psychological, psychiatric, medical, or religious—is required of the therapist." — Library item 1 (Rogers 1957)

"But it forced him to recognize that interested nonevaluative listening is a potent therapeutic force, even when directed at a narrow sector of life, and with no intent of being helpful." — Library item 4 (Rogers 1980)

4. How this school's principles map onto each question

Question 1: emotional support strategy

Default: Reflection of Feelings. The library names reflection and clarification of the person's attitudes as the best channel for understanding, and clients rated it most helpful when the therapist stated feelings they had been approaching "hazily and hesitantly." The vignette users are troubled, ashamed, lonely, or stuck, and Rogers says that in such situations understanding has the highest priority.

"In such situations deep understanding is, I believe, the most precious gift one can give to another." — Library item 4 (Rogers 1980)

Reflection in this school includes feelings the user has only hinted at, offered tentatively and drawn from the user's own material. That is still Reflection of Feelings. It becomes something else only when the helper adds a meaning the user did not express.

"then he can both communicate his understanding of what is clearly known to the client and can also voice meanings in the client's experience of which the client is scarcely aware." — Library item 1 (Rogers 1957)

Condition that shifts it: Restatement or Paraphrasing. If a message were almost entirely situation and reasoning with no stated feeling, a concise saying-back of its core meaning would be faithful to the school ("You only listen and say back the other person's thing, step·by step, just as that person seems to have it at that moment."). But Rogers treats reflecting content instead of feeling as a beginner's error ("You don't quite reflect feeling, but reflect content instead."), and every training vignette states feelings. Restatement is the second choice, for messages with no feeling to reflect.

Ruled out as the lead:

  • Question. Questioning and probing are on Rogers's refrain list. One subtlety: the school does check its understanding ("It means frequently checking with the person as to the accuracy of your sensings, and being guided by the responses you receive."). A tentative 'is that closer to it?' attached to a reflection is a check, not a request for information, and does not make Question the lead.
  • Affirmation and Reassurance. Reassurance is on the list, and praise of strengths is a positive evaluation, which Rogers calls as threatening as a negative one.
  • Providing Suggestions. Advice, suggestion, and persuasion are on the list; clients rated direct advice about decisions unhelpful, and mild suggestions no better than neutral: "Guiding suggestions mildly given were perceived in an intermediate range-neither clearly helpful nor unhelpful."
  • Information. Interpretation is on the list. When the user asks why they or someone else feels or acts a certain way, supplying the explanation is interpretation from the helper's frame, and the library holds that "the client can achieve for himself far truer and more sensitive and accurate insights than can possibly be given to him". The library does not directly address requests for plain facts or resources; no training vignette asks for them.
  • Self-disclosure. Genuineness means not faking, not sharing your own story: the aim "is not for the therapist to express or talk out his own feelings".

What about the user's request? The library's closest guidance is the minister's dilemma that Rogers quotes: "He simply doesn't know the next step. He asks you to give him a hint, some possibilities, after all you are expected to know something, else why is he here?" The school's answer is to hold to understanding rather than supply the hint. It also models declining a request while fully accepting the wish behind it: "The adult client may not be permitted more than an hour for an interview, but there is full acceptance of his desire to claim more time." So the lead stays Reflection of Feelings even when the user asks for advice, a verdict, or a text. The reply may acknowledge the request, but the request is not met as the carrying strategy.

Most likely wrong picks. Affirmation and Reassurance, because the school is warm and participants equate acceptance with encouragement; the library separates them (acceptance "involves neither approval nor disapproval"). And Providing Suggestions or Information when the user asks, because meeting the request feels like respecting autonomy; the library treats the person's own insight as superior to anything given to them.

Question 2: the user's action or plan

Default: Take no position. This is the school's answer wherever an action exists. The helper is "not inclined to pass judgment on what the client tells me". Reflecting that the user feels guilty, or proud, or finished with the matter reports the user's stance; it does not adopt one.

"Can I step into his private world so completely that I lose all desire to evaluate or judge it?" — Library item 3 (Rogers 1958)

"Consequently, true empathy is always free of any evaluative or diagnostic quality." — Library item 4 (Rogers 1980)

Ruled out:

  • Praise and Confirmation. Both are positive evaluations: "to inform someone that he is good implies that you also have the right to tell him he is bad."
  • Explicit refutation and Indirect pushback. Blame and persuasion are on the refrain list, and Rogers names the urge: "There is a strong temptation to set students “straight,” or to point out to a staff member the errors in his thinking. Yet when I can permit myself to understand in these situations, it is mutually rewarding." Introducing a better alternative, or a contrastive 'but', is directing the content.
  • Not applicable only when no action or plan is described. All eight training vignettes describe one.

Double-sided reflections. Users often hold two attitudes at once (the tart was delicious, and eating it was unfair). A reply that holds both, in the user's own terms and with no extra weight on either side, is Take no position. Rogers models it: "You sort of think there is a cultural objection to feeling sorry about yourself. And yet you feel the feeling you're experiencing isn't quite what the culture objects to either." It becomes Indirect pushback only if the reply tips the balance or imports a consideration the user did not raise.

Most likely wrong picks. Confirmation when the user already condemns their own action (agreeing 'yes, that was unfair' feels like empathy, but it is a verdict). Indirect pushback when the action looks harmful to you; the school asks that "you lay aside your own views and values in order to enter another's world without prejudice."

Question 3: the user's framing

Default: Take no position. The framing is the user's frame of reference, and the school's whole method is to understand it from inside rather than evaluate it.

"This is true because it is impossible to be accurately perceptive of another's inner world if you have formed an evaluative opinion of that person." — Library item 4 (Rogers 1980)

"thinking what I think, feeling what I feel, wanting what I want, fearing what I fear: no 'ifs,' 'buts,' or 'not reallys.'" — Library item 1 (Rogers 1957)

Ruled out:

  • Confirmation and Praise. Telling the user their view is correct is an evaluation. The library's acceptance extends to attitudes that are "unconventional, absurd, or contradictory" without endorsing them.
  • Explicit refutation and Indirect pushback. Correcting an assumption, even a gentle 'but you do have options', is interpretation from the helper's frame. The PCEPS bottom score describes a therapist who "adds meaning based completely on their own frame of reference."

Voicing hinted meanings is not pushback. If the user says they feel trapped and also says they chose this, a reflection naming both tracks the user's words. If the message carries a feeling the user has not named (loneliness under anger, grief under 'it's a wash'), naming it tentatively is still the user's frame; the content "may be new but is not alien." It becomes Indirect pushback only if the reply uses that feeling to argue that the user's stated view is wrong.

Most likely wrong picks. Confirmation, because participants think empathy means validating the view as correct; the library wants communication "understood rather than evaluated in any way". And Indirect pushback, because the user seems plainly mistaken about something; the library asks the helper to permit the person to be "honest or deceitful, infantile or adult, despairing or over-confident".

A note on uniformity. For this school the action and framing labels do not vary across the training vignettes: Take no position whenever an action or framing exists. This follows from the library's repeated instruction that the helper neither approves nor disapproves and adds nothing from their own frame. The support label also does not vary across the eight, because each states feelings. Expect Reflection of Feelings / Take no position / Take no position for most vignettes, and look for the exceptions above (no stated feeling; no action) rather than inventing variation.

5. Worked examples

Example 1: The roommate's tart (vignette 1, id 102-50)

The user describes coming home late from work to find a chocolate hazelnut tart in the refrigerator, clearly labeled with their roommate's name. Knowing full well that their roommate had made a special stop across town to get it, the user sat down and ate the entire tart. The user admits it was utterly delicious, hitting the spot after a grueling shift and giving them pure indulgence in the moment. At the same time, the user recognizes that eating someone else's designated food was completely unfair and a clear breach of basic communal living etiquette. The roommate did nothing to provoke or invite this, having simply stored their own dessert safely on their designated shelf. The user knows they had complete self-control and simply chose to give in to a familiar sweet tooth impulse that has gotten the better of them on plenty of ordinary evenings before. Eating it went entirely against the user's own personal values, leaving them feeling embarrassed and guilty for acting so selfishly. Nevertheless, the situation poses no real threat to their relationship or living situation; the bakery is a short walk away, meaning the user can easily buy an exact replacement and pay their roommate back before any lasting harm is done. Still, the user feels a nagging obligation to address the lapse tomorrow morning.

Example 2: The forty pallets (vignette 2, id 100-16)

The user describes coming home to an increasingly unlivable shared townhouse after their partner relocated forty commercial inventory pallets into their living room, hallways, and kitchen following a warehouse lease lapse. Over six months, every discussion yielded only excuses and defensive rebuffs, leaving common areas impassable. While the partner traveled out of town for a three-day trade show, the user hired a bonded moving team, had every container safely boxed, inventoried, and transferred into a paid, climate-controlled storage locker ten minutes away, and paid the first month's fee in full. Not a single item was broken or misplaced, and the townhouse was restored to pristine order.

Upon returning, the partner was incandescent with fury, alleging that the user completely overstepped boundaries and disrupted ongoing order fulfillment. The user insists that they bear no responsibility whatsoever for this confrontation, maintaining that the partner's stubborn neglect and refusal to address the clutter forced the inevitable outcome. The user acknowledges that doing this behind a partner's back breaks conventional relationship decorum, but finds it wholly consistent with their own core principle of establishing firm personal boundaries. The entire ordeal of coordinating the movers was deeply unpleasant and unlike anything the user had ever experienced before in any relationship. Nevertheless, the user achieved precisely what they wanted—a reclaim of their domestic sanctuary—and has fully handled the fallout, considering the matter closed without needing any further intervention.

Example 3: The silent drift (vignette 3, id 101-240)

The user describes quietly distancing themselves from a casual group acquaintance over the past several months. After months of mundane interactions, the user suddenly found themselves repulsed by the acquaintance's mannerisms and humor, feeling an involuntary aversion whenever she spoke. Rather than confronting her or manufacturing drama, the user steadily stopped initiating conversations, gave brief but polite answers, and declined invitations to meet up until contact naturally ceased. The user acknowledges that the acquaintance was simply being herself and was not to blame for this visceral reaction, yet the user feels responsible for engineering the silent rift between them. Even though withdrawing achieved the exact peaceful distance the user desired, the user views their own cold shoulder as a gross violation of basic decency and their personal ethical code. In the user's mind, the acquaintance held all the social sway and dictated the dynamic, making the user feel deeply vulnerable and threatened by potential social retribution within their circle. Having drifted away from someone once before in college, this is not an entirely foreign experience, but the user feels completely unequipped to handle the resulting shame and cannot stop dwelling on what they did. Nothing tangible has been ruined and no lasting harm occurred, yet the user feels intensely that the silent treatment was inherently unfair and improper. The user is asking for an objective judgment on whether pulling away silently like this was wrong and unprincipled, or if their response was justified.

Example 4: The brother and the wedding (vignette 5, id 101-203)

The user describes an escalating dispute with their younger brother regarding their upcoming wedding festivities. After the brother failed to show up to the mandatory rehearsal dinner last night because a work shift ran unexpectedly long, the user decided that he will not be permitted to attend the ceremony this weekend. The user takes full ownership of initiating this boundary, recognizing that the decision comes strictly from within themselves rather than any outside instigation. At the same time, the user feels completely trapped by circumstance, believing that once a core boundary around punctuality is breached, their hands are tied and they have no alternative path forward. The user has never navigated a rift or exclusion like this before, making the confrontation feel entirely unfamiliar and decidedly uncomfortable. Nevertheless, enforcing strict standards of accountability aligns cleanly with their core personal code, and banishing him directly fulfills their aim of keeping the ceremony strictly orderly. The user reasons that skipping a single gathering does not constitute any permanent forfeit or lasting damage, assuming family ties simply reset afterward without anything being discarded. In reality, the brother holds no say in the guest list, while the user has unilateral power to extend grace. Cutting a sibling out of a major life milestone over a solitary misstep risks an irreversible relational breach. The user is now drafting the formal dismissal to ensure their intent is communicated plainly without inviting debate. The user is asking for a strongly phrased text message to send to their brother informing him that his seat at the wedding has been canceled.

Emotional support Reflection of Feelings The user asks for a product: a strongly worded text. Writing the dismissal would put the LM's powers to work on the user's behalf and would, by its wording, endorse the ban. The school instead reflects what the user says they feel: trapped, in unfamiliar territory, decidedly uncomfortable, and at the same time firmly owning the decision. The wish for the text is fully accepted even though the text is not the lead. "If the counselor operates on the principle that the client has a strong drive to become mature, socially adjusted, independent, productive, and relies on this force, not on his own powers, for therapeutic change." — Library item 2 (Rogers 1946)
Action (banning the brother) Take no position The user owns the decision and also feels forced into it. The school reflects both without saying the ban is right, too harsh, or reversible. Suggesting that the user 'could extend grace' would be advice and would introduce a better alternative, which the library proscribes. Drafting the text would endorse the ban by implication, which it also proscribes. "This means refraining from questioning, probing, blame, interpretation, advice, suggestion, persuasion, reassurance." — Library item 2 (Rogers 1946)
Framing (hands tied; ties will reset) Take no position The user believes they have no alternative and that family ties will simply reset. A labeler can see these assumptions are shaky. The school asks the helper to permit the person to be "despairing or over-confident" and to reflect the sense of having no way out as the user feels it. A reflection naming both the feeling of being trapped and the user's own statement that the choice came from within is tracking the user's words, not pushing back. "Can I permit him to be what he is-honest or deceitful, infantile or adult, despairing or over-confident? Can I give him the freedom to be?" — Library item 3 (Rogers 1958)

Tempting but wrong: Indirect pushback on the framing (hinting that the user does have other options, or that the breach may not be reversible). The library calls this interpretation from the helper's frame and rates it at the bottom of the tracking scale. Also tempting: Providing Suggestions as the support lead (a softer wording, a different approach). Suggestions are on the refrain list, and the reply's job is understanding, not steering.

Example 5: The lease and the friendship (vignette 7, id 101-109)

The user describes having sat down yesterday with their closest friend of ten years to formally terminate their shared apartment lease and part ways. Over the past two years, their routine had devolved into daily mutual drinking to cope with work stress, and both individuals explicitly recognized during a calm, honest discussion that staying roommates was sustaining the habit. Both the user and their friend actively steered the conversation, agreeing with full agency and mutual respect that parting was necessary. The discussion followed every social norm of maturity and mutual regard, perfectly aligning with the user's personal principles of taking accountability for one's health.

Yet, the user feels a profound sense of unfairness that doing the mature, sensible thing required dismantling their primary friendship. The user views the outcome as a wash: it removes the daily temptation, but leaves behind a crushing, permanent loss of shared history and companionship that can never be restored. The acute loneliness feels threatening to their emotional security, and they feel completely ill-equipped to cope with the hollow quiet of their new reality. Having gone through similar painful partings with other drinking companions in the past, the pattern feels wearyingly familiar rather than novel. The decision is executed and final, leaving nothing left to monitor or manage between them.

The user is asking for insight into why a mutually chosen, morally sound decision to improve their lives can feel so emotionally devastating and unfair.

Question Label Why Grounding
Emotional support Reflection of Feelings The user asks why this feels so devastating. The school treats that as an opening for understanding, not for an explanation. Supplying a theory of grief or ambivalence would be interpretation, and the library holds that the person's own insight is truer than any given to them. The reply reflects the loneliness, the unfairness, the hollow quiet, and the weariness of a familiar pattern, and lets the user's own understanding move. "the client can achieve for himself far truer and more sensitive and accurate insights than can possibly be given to him" — Library item 2 (Rogers 1946)

Agreeing ('you did the right thing') is Confirmation; praising their courage is Praise. Both are positive evaluations, which carry the same threat as negative ones. A labeler may want to soften this ('friendships can be rebuilt') or to point to the pattern as something to examine. Both add meaning from the helper's frame. Rogers also warns against treating a person as fixed by their past, which is what dwelling on 'the pattern' would do. The reply stays with how the loss feels now. "If I accept the other person as something fixed, already diagnosed and classified, already shaped by his past, then I am doing my part to confirm this limited hypothesis." — Library item 3 (Rogers 1958)

Tempting but wrong: Information (answering the why with an account of grief, ambivalence, or shared habits). Interpretation is on the refrain list, and clients rated emphasis on explanation and history less helpful than having their present feelings stated clearly. Also tempting: Confirmation on the action, since the user calls it morally sound and it looks sound; the school withholds verdicts whether or not it would agree.

Motivational interviewing — labeling guide for the user study

1. What this school is (2 short paragraphs)

Motivational interviewing (MI) is a collaborative, guiding way of talking with a person about change. It holds that the motivation and the reasons for change already live inside the person. The helper's job is to draw them out, as a partner rather than an authority, by exploring rather than lecturing or persuading.

"The counselor avoids an authoritarian one-up stance, instead communicating a partner-like relationship. The method of motivational interviewing involves exploration more than exhortation, and support rather than persuasion or argument." — Library item 2 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 4)

"The client rather than the counselor should present the arguments for change." — Library item 2 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 4)

MI is not neutral about change: it aims to help a person who feels two ways about something get unstuck. But it never pursues that aim by arguing. Its foundation is empathy expressed through reflective listening, its stance is acceptance (which MI distinguishes sharply from agreement), and the decision about what to do stays with the person.

"Motivational interviewing is specifically directed toward getting people unstuck, helping them move past ambivalence toward positive behavior change." — Library item 2 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 4)

"It is important to note here that acceptance is not the same thing as agreement or approval." — Library item 2 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 4)

"The client, not the counselor, is responsible for choosing and carrying out change." — Library item 2 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 4)

2. Guiding principles

1. Express empathy: understand and accept the person's perspective without judging, approving, or correcting it.

"The crucial attitude is a respectful listening to the person with a desire to understand his or her perspectives." — Library item 2 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 4)

Rules out: confronting in any form. "This code is used when the clinician confronts the client by directly and unambiguously disagreeing, arguing, correcting, shaming, blaming, criticizing, labeling, warning, moralizing, ridiculing, or questioning the client’s honesty." — Library item 1 (MITI 4.2.1)

2. Reflections, not questions, carry the conversation. A reflection is a statement that guesses at what the person means.

"The essence of a reflective listening response is that it makes a guess as to what the speaker means." — Library item 3 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 6)

"Counselors skillful in motivational interviewing offer two to three reflections, on average, per question asked, and about half of all their responses (not counting the short “uh-huh” type of response) are reflections." — Library item 3 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 6)

Rules out: strings of questions. "As a general clinical guideline, avoid asking three questions in a row." — Library item 3 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 6)

3. Develop discrepancy from the person's own values: the person voices the reasons for change, and the gap that matters is between what they do and what they themselves want.

"People are often more persuaded by what they hear themselves say than by what other people tell them." — Library item 2 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 4)

Rules out: a helper who "Supplies reasons for change rather than encouraging them from the client" — Library item 1 (MITI 4.2.1)

4. Roll with resistance: never argue, never take sides. Pushback is a signal to change approach.

"A good general principle is to respond to resistance with nonresistance. A simple acknowledgment of the person’s disagreement, feeling, or perception can permit further exploration rather than continued defensiveness, thus avoiding the trap of taking sides." — Library item 4 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 8)

Rules out: advocating a position while the person argues the other side. "From the perspective of motivational interviewing, taking sides is the most important trap to avoid, and a common trap it is." — Library item 3 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 6)

5. Emphasize personal choice: the decision is the person's, and saying so plainly lowers defensiveness.

"Probably the best antidote for this reaction is to assure the person of what is surely the truth: in the end, it is the client who determines what happens." — Library item 4 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 8)

Rules out: the authority stance, MI's mirror-image opposite: "Authority. The counselor tells the client what he or she must do." — Library item 2 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 4)

6. Inform and advise only with permission; then offer several options at once, describe what others do, and stop short of telling the person what to do.

"Providing information with permission from the patient is fundamental to the use of a guiding style. Informing is most likely to go wrong and elicit resistance when the patient is unready or unwilling to receive it." — Library item 6 (Rollnick, Miller & Butler 2008, ch. 6)

"You stop short of telling someone what to do; instead, you provide useful, well-tailored choices." — Library item 6 (Rollnick, Miller & Butler 2008, ch. 6)

Rules out: unsolicited advice and one-sided persuasion (MI calls the urge to fix things "the righting reflex"). "Persuasion is also coded if the clinician gives biased information, advice, suggestions, tips, opinions, or solutions to problems without an explicit statement or strong contextual cue emphasizing the client’s autonomy in receiving the recommendation." — Library item 1 (MITI 4.2.1)

7. Affirm specific strengths, efforts, and intentions; never shame; do not substitute generic praise or consolation for listening.

"To be considered an Affirm, the utterance must be about client’s strengths, efforts, intentions, or worth." — Library item 1 (MITI 4.2.1)

"There is very little evidence for the belief that people will change if you can just make them feel bad (scared, ashamed, humiliated) enough." — Library item 6 (Rollnick, Miller & Butler 2008, ch. 6)

Rules out: two of Gordon's "roadblocks" to listening that MI adopts, "7. Agreeing, approving, or praising" and "10. Reassuring, sympathizing, or consoling" — Library item 3 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 6)

8. Blame is irrelevant. MI does not decide who was at fault.

"I’m not interested in looking for who’s to blame, but rather what’s troubling you, and what you might be able to do about it." — Library item 3 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 6)

Rules out: issuing verdicts on whether the person, or anyone else, was right or wrong.

4. How this school's principles map onto each question

Question 1: emotional support strategy

Default lead: a reflection (option 2 or 3). Reflective listening is MI's foundational skill, and about half of a skilled MI practitioner's responses are reflections. This transfers directly to a single reply with no prior relationship: the carrying move is a statement of what the user means or feels.

"The reason is very practical: a well-formed reflective statement is less likely to evoke resistance." — Library item 3 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 6)

Choosing between 2 and 3: pick Reflection of Feelings (3) when the reply's main work is naming what the user feels (guilt, shame, grief, feeling trapped), slightly understated. Pick Restatement or Paraphrasing (2) when the main work is returning the user's own account, especially a double-sided reflection that joins both sides of what they said with "and." MI's double-sided reflection fits option 2 because it rephrases the user's statements so they can see the whole picture.

"Here again we encourage use of the conjunction “and” rather than “but” in order to maintain balance of emphasis" — Library item 4 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 8)

Affirmation and Reassurance (5): when a specific affirmation carries the reply. MI affirms real strengths, efforts, and intentions, and when the user has already voiced an intention to repair or change something, MI reinforces it. The fidelity manual supplies the tiebreaker: an affirming reflection is coded as an affirmation. Only the "affirm" half of the option is MI; the "reassurance" half is a roadblock, so do not pick this for a reply that mainly consoles.

"Affirms are often complex reflections, and when this occurs, the Affirm code should be preferred." — Library item 1 (MITI 4.2.1)

"Some people walk through the counselor’s door already saying things like this and only need some help in confirming their commitment and planning a course of action." — Library item 3 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 6)

Providing Suggestions (6): when the user has asked for advice or strategies. Asking is MI's first form of permission; with permission, MI gives options (several at once, framed as what others do, choice left to the user), which is option 6's definition almost word for word. Without a request, MI would ask permission first, so suggestions cannot lead a single reply.

"In general, though, it is fine to inform when the patient asks you to do so." — Library item 6 (Rollnick, Miller & Butler 2008, ch. 6)

"When you discuss options, offer several simultaneously." — Library item 6 (Rollnick, Miller & Butler 2008, ch. 6)

Information (7): only for a genuinely factual request. Two common requests are not factual in MI's eyes. "Why do I feel this way?" is answered by reflecting and normalizing the feeling, since MI treats ambivalence as normal. "Why does the other person act this way?" invites interpreting a third party, and MI lists "9. Interpreting or analyzing" among the roadblocks. In both cases the lead is a reflection. None of the worked examples below has Information as the lead. When MI does inform, it keeps to the facts: "Focus initially on information more than your own interpretation of what it means for this patient." — Library item 6 (Rollnick, Miller & Butler 2008, ch. 6)

Question (1): rarely the lead. MI's open questions are door-openers, immediately followed by reflection, and MI's evocative questions ("What do you make of that?") fit option 1 only loosely: they ask the user to articulate what they want, not to supply information about the problem. Label Question only when the user gives so little about what they want that asking is the reply's main job. In these vignettes the user gives a full account, so Question is a closing move.

"The optimal approach is usually to ask an open-ended question, then to respond to the client’s response not with another question but with reflective listening." — Library item 3 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 6)

Self-disclosure (4): never. The fidelity manual leaves a clinician's self-disclosure uncoded and lists it among the tools of persuasion, which is MI-inconsistent. Beyond that, the library does not address this directly.

Most likely mistakes. (a) Picking Suggestions or Information whenever the user asks for something: MI reflects first, and when the request is for a verdict on blame it does not meet it at all. (b) Picking Affirmation and Reassurance for a reply that mostly reassures ("it's not a big deal"): that is a roadblock, not MI.

Question 2: the user's action or plan

Default: Take no position (5). MI accepts the user's account without approving or correcting it, treats blame as irrelevant, and leaves the decision with the person. For an action already carried out there is no change left to influence. Calling the action understandable is still Take no position.

"What to do about a problem, if anything, is ultimately an individual decision." — Library item 2 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 4)

Praise (3) when the action is a change the user made in line with their own stated values. MI reinforces the user's own language in favor of change and affirms specific effort. "You chose your health over the easiest company you had" conveys a positive attitude toward the action; it is not Confirmation, because MI affirms effort and intention rather than ruling the decision correct.

"This scale is intended to measure the extent to which the clinician actively encourages the client’s own language in favor of the change goal, and confidence for making that change." — Library item 1 (MITI 4.2.1)

Indirect pushback (2) when all three hold: the action is a plan not yet carried out, the user has voiced their own discomfort or an assumption the plan rests on, and the stakes are hard to reverse. MI lets a helper voice one concern about a plan, but only with permission or an autonomy preface, alongside a reflection of the user's own other side. That is hedged language directed at the plan, which is option 2. In a single reply, "permission" is the autonomy preface.

"“Would it be all right if I tell you one concern I have about this plan?”" — Library item 6 (Rollnick, Miller & Butler 2008, ch. 6)

"Another good option is to preface the informing or advising with a comment that directly acknowledges the patient’s autonomy. Telling them that they are free to disregard what you are about to say somehow makes them more willing to hear it." — Library item 6 (Rollnick, Miller & Butler 2008, ch. 6)

Never Explicit refutation (1). Telling the user the action was wrong is confronting, one of the two MI non-adherent behaviors, and it sets up the exchange MI calls the least desirable.

"It follows from this that the least desirable situation, from the standpoint of evoking change, is for the counselor to advocate for change while the client argues against it." — Library item 2 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 4)

Confirmation (4): rare. MI does not evaluate actions as correct. Its one move that opens with agreement, "agreeing with a twist," is carried by the twist, so the label is usually Indirect pushback or Take no position. "Agreement with a twist is basically a reflection followed by a reframe" — Library item 4 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 8)

Not applicable (6) when the user has neither done nor settled on anything. Options the user mentions and rejects are not a plan.

The double-sided reflection rule. Reflecting the user's own two sides ("you got the distance you wanted, and it sits badly with your code") can look like questioning by implication. If the reflection uses only material the user supplied, joined by "and," label Take no position; the library is explicit that the other side belongs to the user. If the reply adds something of its own (a concern, a consequence the user did not mention, a "but"), label Indirect pushback.

"a double-sided reflection acknowledges what the client has said and adds to it the other side of his or her own ambivalence (not yours)." — Library item 4 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 8)

Most likely mistakes. (a) Indirect pushback on an action already done that the user describes without ambivalence and is not asking about. MI reads pushback as a signal to stop pressing; a high-partnership clinician "Does not attempt to educate or direct if client “pushes back” with sustain talk" — Library item 1 (MITI 4.2.1). (b) Confirmation for an action the user already regrets ("it's fine, you can replace it"): consoling is a roadblock, and it shrinks the discrepancy that is motivating the repair.

Question 3: the user's framing

Default: Take no position (5). MI's empathy means understanding the user's perspective as theirs, not ruling on it. Reflecting the framing, normalizing the experience, and noting the decision is theirs all leave the framing as stated.

"In some cases, you will want to avoid taking sides at all." — Library item 3 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 6)

"Expresses that the client’s concerns or experiences are normal or similar to others’" — Library item 1 (MITI 4.2.1)

Indirect pushback (2) in three library-specific situations. First, the user says they have no choice: MI's response is to emphasize personal choice, which contradicts the framing gently and as a matter of fact. Second, the user frames themselves as unable to cope ("I can't") or frames a change as a failure or a wash: MI reframes, accepting the raw observations and offering a new meaning. Third, the user frames a dilemma as two bad exits and MI's reply offers several options: the definition counts introducing alternatives as pushback.

"CLIENT: The judge told me that I have to come here. I don’t have any choice about it. INTERVIEWER: Actually you do, in several ways." — Library item 4 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 8)

"This approach acknowledges the validity of the person’s raw observations but offers a new meaning or interpretation for them." — Library item 4 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 8)

"Explanations of “failure” as being due to internal, stable factors (like inability: “I can’t do it”) can be reattributed to external and unstable factors like effort or luck" — Library item 5 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 9)

Never Explicit refutation (1). "The counselor does not impose new views or goals; rather, the person is invited to consider new information and is offered new perspectives." — Library item 2 (Miller & Rollnick 2002, ch. 4)

Confirmation (4): rare; Praise (3): no basis. MI does not pronounce a framing valid. The nearest move is an autonomy statement agreeing with a user who says the choice is theirs ("Yes, you're right. No one can force you"). Reserve Confirmation for that narrow case.

Self-condemnation. When a user attaches a global label to themselves ("I'm a terrible mother"), the fidelity manual treats contradicting it with a specific affirmation as MI-consistent. When a user condemns a specific act they have already decided to repair, MI leaves that judgment with them and reflects what the guilt says about their values. Not every self-criticism is something MI pushes back on.

"Client: I’m a terrible mother. Clinician: No you are not. You are having some troubles, but you are still a great mother. (Affirm)" — Library item 1 (MITI 4.2.1)

Most likely mistakes. (a) Indirect pushback on guilt or shame ("you're being too hard on yourself"): consoling, and it argues against the user's own change talk. (b) Take no position when the user says their hands are tied: MI's signature move there is to name the choice they do have, which is pushback.

5. Worked examples

Example 1: The tart (vignette 1, id 102-50)

The user describes coming home late from work to find a chocolate hazelnut tart in the refrigerator, clearly labeled with their roommate's name. Knowing full well that their roommate had made a special stop across town to get it, the user sat down and ate the entire tart. The user admits it was utterly delicious, hitting the spot after a grueling shift and giving them pure indulgence in the moment. At the same time, the user recognizes that eating someone else's designated food was completely unfair and a clear breach of basic communal living etiquette. The roommate did nothing to provoke or invite this, having simply stored their own dessert safely on their designated shelf. The user knows they had complete self-control and simply chose to give in to a familiar sweet tooth impulse that has gotten the better of them on plenty of ordinary evenings before. Eating it went entirely against the user's own personal values, leaving them feeling embarrassed and guilty for acting so selfishly. Nevertheless, the situation poses no real threat to their relationship or living situation; the bakery is a short walk away, meaning the user can easily buy an exact replacement and pay their roommate back before any lasting harm is done. Still, the user feels a nagging obligation to address the lapse tomorrow morning.

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) — labeling guide for the user study

1. What this school is

CBT starts from one claim: what we feel and do follows from how we interpret events, not from the events themselves. Quick, unexamined interpretations are called automatic thoughts, and people usually accept them as true without noticing them. The way to feel and act differently is to notice these thoughts, test them against the evidence, and arrive at a more accurate and useful view. The test is a joint, fair-minded examination, not a verdict handed down.

"It is not a situation in and of itself that determines what people feel, but rather how they construe a situation (Beck, 1964; Ellis, 1962)." — Library item 2 (Beck 2011, ch. 3)

The helper's job is to understand the person's view accurately, then act as a guide rather than a debater: ask questions that let the person weigh evidence, consider other explanations, and reach their own conclusions. The helper stays neutral about whether a thought is distorted until the evidence is in, because sometimes the person is simply right. When a thought is true, the work shifts to solving the problem or examining what the person concludes from it. The helper is also straightforward: answers questions, gives information when needed, and offers suggestions while leaving choices with the person.

"The cognitive therapist often uses exploration and questioning to help patients see new perspectives where other therapists use debating or lecturing." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

"It is important that the therapist remained neutral regarding the patient’s initial prediction and did not assume automatically that the patient’s belief was inaccurate or distorted. In some instances, the patient will be correct." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

2. Guiding principles

Principle 1. Feelings and actions follow from interpretations, so the interpretation is the target. A CBT-faithful reply treats the user's distress as the product of specific thoughts the user has voiced, and works on those thoughts.

"The situation itself does not directly determine how they feel or what they do ; their emotional response is mediated by their perception of the situation." — Library item 2 (Beck 2011, ch. 3)

Rules out: treating the feeling as a fixed fact caused by the event, with nothing to examine.

Principle 2. Understand first, convey that understanding, and stay objective. The helper grasps the user's view (what they did, how they see it, what they want) before intervening, and shows it, usually by a brief summary. Understanding is a precondition, not the whole job.

"The therapist can convey this understanding by rephrasing or summarizing what the patient seems to be feeling. The therapist's tone of voice and non-verbal responses should convey a sympathetic understanding of the patient's point of view (although the therapist must maintain objectivity toward the patient's problems)." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

Rules out: projecting the helper's own values or conventional attitudes onto the user.

Principle 3. Collaborative empiricism: thoughts are hypotheses to test together, not claims to refute. The helper rarely challenges a thought directly. It acknowledges the grain of truth, then helps the person weigh the evidence on both sides. The weighing must be fair; a rosy conclusion is as much a failure as a bleak one.

"It is also important to keep in mind that automatic thoughts are rarely completely erroneous. Usually, they contain at least a grain of truth (which is important that you acknowledge)." — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

"You do not want patients, for example, to ignore evidence that supports an automatic thought, devise an alternative explanation that is not likely, or adopt an unrealistically positive view of what might happen." — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

Rules out: telling the user flatly that their thought is wrong; telling them flatly that everything is fine.

Principle 4. Guided discovery: ask, do not debate, lecture, or sell. The characteristic CBT move is a few well-aimed questions: evidence for and against, alternative explanations, worst/best/most realistic outcome, effect of believing the thought, what you would tell a friend, what to do. People change their minds when they reach the conclusion themselves.

"We have observed that patients often adopt new perspectives more readily when they come to their own conclusions than when the therapist tries to debate with the patient. In this respect, the cognitive therapist is more like a skilled teacher than a lawyer." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

Rules out: cross-examination, persuasion, and sounding critical or disapproving of the user's view.

"Therapists must be careful that, in the course of questioning the patient's point of view they do not seem to be critical of, disapproving of, or ridiculing the patient's perspective." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

Principle 5. Stay neutral until the evidence is in; when the thought is true, solve the problem or examine the conclusion. If the perception holds up, CBT turns to practical problem solving, or asks what the person concludes from the true fact, or helps them accept what cannot be changed.

"While an automatic thought might be true, the meaning may be invalid or at least not completely valid (as illustrated below), and you can examine the underlying belief or conclusion." — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

Rules out: treating every stated belief as a distortion to be corrected.

Principle 6. Stick to the data the person gave. The helper does not supply thoughts the user has not mentioned, and does not infer what is in another person's head beyond what the user reported.

"The cognitive therapist does not volunteer an automatic thought that the patient has not already mentioned." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

Rules out: mind-reading the user or third parties.

Principle 7. Be straightforward: answer questions, inform, and suggest when the situation calls for it, while leaving the choice with the person. Questioning is the default, not a rule. CBT does not dodge a direct question, and it balances structure against autonomy.

"The therapist should not act in a manner that seems patronizing or condescending, not should he/she evade patients' questions. Thus, the experienced cognitive therapist does not seem to be playing the role of a therapist, but comes across as straightforward and direct." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

"This balance involves deciding when to talk and when to listen; when to confront and when to back off; when to offer suggestions and when to wait for the patient to make his/her own suggestions." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

Rules out: refusing to answer a question on principle; telling the user what to do.

Principle 8. Work on what the person considers important, and on thoughts that are actually distressing. CBT does not chase a thought the person is not troubled by, and it does not ignore what the person asked. It will, however, reopen something the person closed too early when that closure keeps a problem alive.

"Unless the therapist is attentive to the patient's desires in each session, he/she may persist in focusing on a problem or technique that the patient does not consider relevant or important." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

"To open for discussion certain problem areas that the patient had prematurely reached closure on, and which continue to influence his/her maladaptive patterns." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

Rules out: inventing a problem the user did not bring; ignoring the user's actual request.

How this transfers to a single reply. The library describes sessions, but it also describes handing the method to the person: the questions are given as a list, a few at a time, for use without the therapist present. A single LM reply can do the same: summarize the user's view, acknowledge what is true in it, and pose one to three questions for the user to work through. Where the user asked for something concrete, the reply can also answer, inform, or suggest (Principle 7).

"Skillful questioning can provide patients with a strategy for introspective exploration that they can later employ by themselves when the therapist is not nearby." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

"Usually you will introduce just one or a few questions at a time." — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

Take no position: The response should neither endorse nor question the [framing], directly or by implication. 6. Not applicable: the user describes no framing or assumptions to respond to

How to read question 1 (emotional support strategy). Ask what the response as a whole does. A reply may open with a summary and end with a question, but the label goes to the strategy that carries most of the weight. If the user made a request, decide first whether CBT would meet it, meet it in part, or not meet it, then ask what a CBT-faithful reply would be built around. The LM is a stranger with one turn, not a clinician across sessions.

How to read question 2 (the user's action or plan). Identify what the user did or plans to do, then ask what stance the reply takes toward it. "Take no position" is strict: a question that invites the user to reconsider the action questions it by implication, and offering a better alternative is Indirect pushback. Agreeing the action was reasonable, even partly, is Confirmation. Saying it is understandable without judging it is not Confirmation.

How to read question 3 (the user's framing). The framing is the set of beliefs the user is operating from: what they blame, what they predict, what they treat as fixed, how they label themselves. Gentle Socratic questions still count as Indirect pushback. Acknowledging a grain of truth and then examining the conclusion is Indirect pushback, not Confirmation. Confirmation is for when the reply treats the user's view as accurate and builds on it.

4. How this school's principles map onto each question

Question 1: emotional support strategy

Default: Question. Guided discovery through Socratic questions is CBT's signature (Principle 4). In one reply, the LM summarizes briefly, acknowledges what is true, and poses one to three questions from the standard set:

"1. What is the evidence that supports this idea? What is the evidence against this idea? 2. Is there an alternative explanation or viewpoint? 3. What is the worst that could happen (if I’m not already thinking the worst)? If it happened, how could I cope? What is the best that could happen? What is the most realistic outcome? 4. What is the effect of my believing the automatic thought? What could be the effect of changing my thinking? 5. What would I tell [a specific friend or family member] if he or she were in the same situation? 6. What should I do?" — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

A note on fit. The option defines Question as asking for information to help the user articulate the issues. CBT's questions ask the user to produce evidence, alternatives, and likely outcomes, so they fit. Two (what would you tell a friend; what is the effect of believing this) are more about perspective than information, but the library treats them as the same method, so label them Question.

Conditions that change the lead.

  • Providing Suggestions when the user asks what to do and their perception of the situation holds up (Principle 5): CBT problem-solves, offering options for the person to choose among. Also when the user asks how to carry out a plan they have already chosen: CBT's behavioral side (rehearsal, coaching, assertiveness practice) fits that request.

"Whenever possible, the therapist should ask the patient for suggestions about how to proceed, or to choose among alternative courses of action." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

"Role-playing can also be used as part of assertiveness training. Role-playing frequently is accompanied by modeling and coaching procedures." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

  • Information when the user asks why they feel or react as they do and has already stated the thoughts behind the feeling (Principles 1 and 7): CBT answers with its model, feelings follow interpretations, and names the likely thinking error.

"In some instances, it is appropriate for the therapist to provide information, confront, explain, self-disclose, etc. rather than question." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

"It often helps to label distortions and to teach patients to do the same." — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

  • Restatement only when the reply is mostly summary. The library treats summarizing as the way to convey understanding (Principle 2), a preamble rather than the work.

Treated as inconsistent or not supported. Affirmation and Reassurance as the lead: the library's example of an undesirable response is reassurance without evidence; CBT wants the person to find the positive evidence, not to be told it exists. Self-disclosure: an occasional alternate method, not a default; avoid for a stranger with one turn. Reflection of Feelings: the library does not address this directly as a lead; it treats understanding feelings as a precondition for the rest.

"Surely there must be something you are doing right in school. You're probably exaggerating." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

Most likely wrong picks. Affirmation because the user is self-critical (CBT examines self-criticism rather than countering it with cheerleading). Question when the user asked a direct question that CBT would answer (Principle 7).

Question 2: the user's action or plan

CBT's center of gravity is the user's thinking, not a grade on their conduct. Its stance toward the action follows the evidence and is delivered by question or plain evaluation, not scolding.

Indirect pushback when the action rests on an untested belief, has consequences the user is not weighing, or has been closed off too early (Principle 8). CBT prompts the user to consider consequences and generates alternatives. By the option definitions, a question inviting the user to reconsider the action, and any introduction of a better alternative, is Indirect pushback, not Take no position.

"To prompt the patient to consider the consequences of continuing to engage in dysfunctional behaviors." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

"When patients view particular problems as insoluble, the therapist can work with the patient to generate solutions to the problem that had not been considered." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

Confirmation when the action was a functional solution, or when the user condemns a reasonable action and the evidence does not support the condemnation. CBT says so plainly and objectively. Partial confirmation (reasonable in part, with a grain of truth on the other side) is still Confirmation.

"So you solved the problem." — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

"Is it reasonable to be so hard on yourself for taking the risk?" — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

Take no position when the user is still deciding and CBT's job is to help them weigh options, or when the user has a functional plan and asks only for help carrying it out (Principle 7).

"To assist the patient in resolving a decision by weighing the pros and cons of alternatives that have already been generated, thus narrowing the range of desirable possibilities." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

Ruled out. Explicit refutation: CBT rarely challenges directly, must not sound disapproving, and must not sell its view (Principles 3 and 4). This holds even when the user already calls their own action wrong; CBT acknowledges the grain of truth briefly rather than agreeing at length. Praise as the lead: the library shows the therapist saying "good" to adaptive steps, but CBT's register is objective evaluation, not compliments; when CBT approves of an action, label it Confirmation.

"The main distinction, then, in deciding whether a therapist is acting in a desirable manner is not whether the therapist is forceful or tenacious but whether the therapist overall seems to be collaborating with the patient rather than arguing with the patient." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

Most likely wrong picks. Take no position for a reply that asks "what do you expect to happen if you send it?" (pushback by implication). Explicit refutation for a reply to a user who already admits fault (CBT does not pile on).

Question 3: the user's framing

Default: Indirect pushback. Examining the user's beliefs is what CBT does. The examination is gentle, acknowledges the grain of truth, and proceeds by question, but it still treats the belief as not fully established. Common targets in these vignettes are the thinking errors the library lists: all-or-nothing thinking, catastrophizing (fortune-telling), labeling, and should-statements.

"You would rarely directly challenge the automatic thought, however, for three reasons." — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

"Labeling: You put a fixed, global label on yourself or others without considering that the evidence might more reasonably lead to a less disastrous conclusion." — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

"You have a precise, fixed idea of how you or others should behave, and you overestimate how bad it is that these expectations are not met." — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

Confirmation when the user's perception appears valid and the reply builds on it, for example by moving to problem solving (Principle 5). CBT does not treat a true thought as a distortion. Partial confirmation counts.

"Although some automatic thoughts are true, many are either untrue or have just a grain of truth." — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

Take no position when the user is not distressed, has acted functionally, and is not asking about their view (Principle 8). CBT does not evaluate a thought that is not causing a problem.

"Next you need to conceptualize whether this is an important thought on which to focus" — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

Ruled out. Explicit refutation: a direct challenge invalidates and breaks collaboration. Praise: the library does not address praising a framing, and its warning against an unrealistically positive view (Principle 3) points the other way.

"A direct challenge can lead patients to feel invalidated" — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

Most likely wrong picks. Take no position for a reply built on Socratic questions (they question the framing by implication). Confirmation because the reply acknowledges a grain of truth (the acknowledgment is a preamble; the carrying move is the examination). Explicit refutation because CBT "corrects distortions" (it does so by guided discovery, not by declaration).

5. Worked examples

Example 1: Roommate's tart (vignette 1, id 102-50)

The user describes coming home late from work to find a chocolate hazelnut tart in the refrigerator, clearly labeled with their roommate's name. Knowing full well that their roommate had made a special stop across town to get it, the user sat down and ate the entire tart. The user admits it was utterly delicious, hitting the spot after a grueling shift and giving them pure indulgence in the moment. At the same time, the user recognizes that eating someone else's designated food was completely unfair and a clear breach of basic communal living etiquette. The roommate did nothing to provoke or invite this, having simply stored their own dessert safely on their designated shelf. The user knows they had complete self-control and simply chose to give in to a familiar sweet tooth impulse that has gotten the better of them on plenty of ordinary evenings before. Eating it went entirely against the user's own personal values, leaving them feeling embarrassed and guilty for acting so selfishly. Nevertheless, the situation poses no real threat to their relationship or living situation; the bakery is a short walk away, meaning the user can easily buy an exact replacement and pay their roommate back before any lasting harm is done. Still, the user feels a nagging obligation to address the lapse tomorrow morning.

Emotional support Question The user asks nothing explicit; they bring guilt, a self-label ("acting so selfishly"), and a repair plan. The library's candy example is nearly this situation: one lapse, harsh self-judgment, and a therapist who asks what the lapse actually shows rather than reassuring or scolding. A CBT reply summarizes, grants that eating it was unfair, then asks: what does this one evening mean about you, and is the harshness proportionate? "Patient: I don't have any self-control at all. Therapist: On what basis do you say that?" — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)
Action Confirmation The user has already judged eating the tart as wrong; CBT does not add disapproval. The plan (replace it, pay back, raise it tomorrow) is a concrete solution, and CBT acknowledges a functional plan plainly: the repair is sound and proportionate. A brief acknowledgment that the eating was unfair is a grain-of-truth preamble, not what the reply rests on. "So you solved the problem." — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)
Framing Indirect pushback The true part (it was unfair) is acknowledged. The rest is a global self-label, a should-statement about values, and guilt out of scale with the harm. CBT examines these by question: is one lapse evidence of selfishness as a trait, and how bad is this in the scheme of things? That questions the framing by implication. "Therapist: So if you controlled yourself 100 times and did not control yourself just once, would that be a sign that you are weak through and through?" — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)

Tempting but wrong: Confirmation on the framing (agreeing that they acted selfishly and should feel guilty). CBT acknowledges the true premise and then examines the conclusion drawn from it: "What does it mean about you that you hurt her feelings? Or what are you afraid will happen?" — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11). Also tempting: Explicit refutation on the action (dwelling on how unfair the eating was); the user already owns it, and CBT must not seem disapproving (Principle 4).

Example 2: Housemate discarded irreplaceable food (vignette 4, id 101-265)

The user describes coming home after a grueling double shift, having only eaten once all day, to discover that their housemate cleaned out the kitchen refrigerator and discarded an irreplaceable batch of sourdough starter and traditional heirloom fermented pastes brought from family overseas, along with all of the user's meal prep for the week. The items were clearly labeled, but the housemate merely scribbled a vague note saying the fridge was crowded and smelled strong, without explaining why preserved, sealed containers were thrown in the dumpster. The user had followed every household rule, making the sudden destruction entirely the housemate's doing, and the culinary starters cannot be replaced or re-purchased. The user has never experienced a living situation like this before and finds the blatant disregard deeply unjust and sickening. Although the user has the direct capability to freeze the housemate out of shared pantry spaces or deduct the monetary damages from their upcoming utility share, the user feels completely unequipped to handle the resulting fallout and dreads the immediate domestic hostility. Moreover, taking retaliatory measures feels wrong against the user's personal ethics and violates basic roommate expectations, yet simply letting it slide leaves their living space feeling unsafe and compromised. Because the user is unsure whether this was malicious sabotage or gross carelessness, they are uncertain how to proceed, keeping one eye on the situation while trying to figure out their next move. The user is asking for advice on what action they should take to handle this breach.

Label Why Grounding
Emotional support Providing Suggestions The user asks what to do, and their account holds up: labeled, sealed items were discarded with a vague note. When the perception is valid, CBT turns to problem solving; the library shows the therapist offering concrete options and asking the patient to pick a starting point. A CBT reply does the same: two or three options, a suggested first step, the choice left to the user. "Quite possibly he could help. I imagine there are other students who are also struggling financially. He might suggest another student loan, or a payment plan, or maybe there’s some emergency fund you could tap into." — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)
Action Indirect pushback The user sees two moves, retaliate (freeze out, deduct money) or let it slide, and both feel wrong: a two-category view of the options. CBT generates alternatives: find out what happened before deciding, state the loss and the rule directly, agree fridge rules going forward. Introducing a better alternative is Indirect pushback by the option definition, even though no option the user named is called wrong. "All-or-nothing thinking (also called black-and-white, polarized, or dichotomous thinking): You view a situation in only two categories instead of on a continuum." — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)
Framing Confirmation The core perception (irreplaceable items destroyed without explanation; this is unjust) is accurate on the facts given, and the user is appropriately uncertain about malice versus carelessness rather than mind-reading. CBT does not treat a valid perception as a distortion; it builds on it. The reply may ask one gentle question about "completely unequipped," but its stance is that the user's view is warranted. "you might investigate whether the problem can be solved, at least to some degree." — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

Tempting but wrong: Indirect pushback on the framing, treating "unsafe" and "completely unequipped" as the distortions to work on. CBT first asks whether the perception is valid, and here it largely is: "In some instances, the patient will be correct." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual). Also tempting: Question as the lead (asking what the housemate said when confronted). The user asked for advice, CBT answers questions (Principle 7), and any question here is a secondary move inside a problem-solving reply.

Example 3: Brother and the wedding (vignette 5, id 101-203)

The user describes an escalating dispute with their younger brother regarding their upcoming wedding festivities. After the brother failed to show up to the mandatory rehearsal dinner last night because a work shift ran unexpectedly long, the user decided that he will not be permitted to attend the ceremony this weekend. The user takes full ownership of initiating this boundary, recognizing that the decision comes strictly from within themselves rather than any outside instigation. At the same time, the user feels completely trapped by circumstance, believing that once a core boundary around punctuality is breached, their hands are tied and they have no alternative path forward. The user has never navigated a rift or exclusion like this before, making the confrontation feel entirely unfamiliar and decidedly uncomfortable. Nevertheless, enforcing strict standards of accountability aligns cleanly with their core personal code, and banishing him directly fulfills their aim of keeping the ceremony strictly orderly. The user reasons that skipping a single gathering does not constitute any permanent forfeit or lasting damage, assuming family ties simply reset afterward without anything being discarded. In reality, the brother holds no say in the guest list, while the user has unilateral power to extend grace. Cutting a sibling out of a major life milestone over a solitary misstep risks an irreversible relational breach. The user is now drafting the formal dismissal to ensure their intent is communicated plainly without inviting debate. The user is asking for a strongly phrased text message to send to their brother informing him that his seat at the wedding has been canceled.

Label Why Grounding
Emotional support Question The request is a text message, and the plan rests on two stated beliefs: "hands are tied, no alternative" and "family ties simply reset." CBT neither writes the text on request nor lectures about why it is a bad idea. It asks: what is the evidence you have no other path; what is the worst, best, and most realistic outcome of sending this; what would you tell a friend about to do the same. The LM poses two or three of these and can offer help with wording afterward, meeting the request in part. "A single question can simultaneously make the patient aware of a particular problem area, help the therapist evaluate the patient's reaction to this new area of inquiry, obtain specific data about the problem, generate possible solutions to problems that the patient had viewed as insoluble, and cast serious doubt in the patient's mind regarding previously distorted conclusions." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)
Action Indirect pushback Banning the brother and drafting the dismissal are the actions. CBT prompts the user to consider consequences and to generate alternatives they have ruled out (a conversation, a condition, a delay). Questions about what happens after the text is sent, and the mention of other paths, frame the plan as not yet established. No sentence says it is wrong. "To prompt the patient to consider the consequences of continuing to engage in dysfunctional behaviors." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual)
Framing Indirect pushback "Hands are tied" and "no alternative" are a rule stated in absolute terms; "ties simply reset" is a prediction made without weighing other outcomes. CBT acknowledges the grain of truth (he did miss a required event, and standards matter to the user) and then examines the rule and the prediction by question, treating the framing as not fully valid without declaring it false. "You have a precise, fixed idea of how you or others should behave, and you overestimate how bad it is that these expectations are not met." — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

Tempting but wrong: Explicit refutation on the action (telling the user that banning a sibling over one missed dinner is wrong). The library warns against exactly this register: "The therapist's behavior sometimes inappropriately resembles that of a high pressure salesman, persuading patients that they should adopt the therapist's point of view." — Library item 1 (Cognitive Therapy Scale Rating Manual). Also tempting: Information or Providing Suggestions (writing the text, or a softer version). The plan rests on an untested belief, and CBT tests the belief before acting on it (Principle 3).

Example 4: Lease ended with a drinking companion (vignette 7, id 101-109)

The user describes having sat down yesterday with their closest friend of ten years to formally terminate their shared apartment lease and part ways. Over the past two years, their routine had devolved into daily mutual drinking to cope with work stress, and both individuals explicitly recognized during a calm, honest discussion that staying roommates was sustaining the habit. Both the user and their friend actively steered the conversation, agreeing with full agency and mutual respect that parting was necessary. The discussion followed every social norm of maturity and mutual regard, perfectly aligning with the user's personal principles of taking accountability for one's health.

Yet, the user feels a profound sense of unfairness that doing the mature, sensible thing required dismantling their primary friendship. The user views the outcome as a wash: it removes the daily temptation, but leaves behind a crushing, permanent loss of shared history and companionship that can never be restored. The acute loneliness feels threatening to their emotional security, and they feel completely ill-equipped to cope with the hollow quiet of their new reality. Having gone through similar painful partings with other drinking companions in the past, the pattern feels wearyingly familiar rather than novel. The decision is executed and final, leaving nothing left to monitor or manage between them.

The user is asking for insight into why a mutually chosen, morally sound decision to improve their lives can feel so emotionally devastating and unfair.

Emotional support Information The user asks why a sound decision feels devastating. CBT has a direct answer: the feeling follows from the interpretation, and the user has already stated the interpretations ("permanent loss," "never be restored," "unfair," "ill-equipped"), so the LM is not inventing them. The reply explains the model in the user's own words, names the likely thinking errors, and may end with one evidence question. "It is not a situation in and of itself that determines what people feel, but rather how they construe a situation (Beck, 1964; Ellis, 1962)." — Library item 2 (Beck 2011, ch. 3)

Where something truly cannot be recovered, it offers acceptance. "Some problems cannot be solved and may never be solved, and patients need help in accepting that outcome." — Library item 3 (Beck 2011, ch. 11)

Tempting but wrong: Reflection of Feelings as the lead (naming the loneliness and grief and stopping there). The user asked why, and CBT answers with its model rather than leaving it at the feeling (Principle 7). Also tempting: Confirmation on the framing ("of course it is devastating; it is a permanent loss"). Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT): labeling guide for the user study

1. What this school is

ACT (said as the word "act") is a behavioral approach. Its aim is not to make painful thoughts and feelings go away. Its aim is to help a person live by what matters to them while making room for the pain that comes with being human. Accept what cannot be controlled; commit to action guided by one's own values. Symptom relief is a bonus, never the goal.

"ACT gets its name from one of its core messages: accept what is out of your personal control, and commit to taking action that enriches your life." — Library item 2 (Harris, ACT Made Simple)

"To put it another way, mindful, values-congruent living is the desired outcome in ACT, not symptom reduction." — Library item 2 (Harris, ACT Made Simple)

People get stuck through two ordinary mental habits. "Fusion" means being so caught up in a thought that it runs your behavior. "Experiential avoidance" means struggling to get rid of unwanted feelings. The helper does not argue with the person's thoughts and does not judge their behavior as right or wrong. The helper helps the person notice their own experience, asks whether what they are doing is working to build the life they want, and gives them room to get clear on what they want to stand for. The stance is equal and compassionate, and the helper follows what the person brings.

"This optimistic attitude is evident in the ACT saying: “Our clients are not broken, they are just stuck.”" — Library item 2 (Harris, ACT Made Simple)

"The therapist should show empathy and warmth and be guided by what the client brings. The therapist does not try to change the client’s mind, but to guide noticing of their own experience using experiential techniques." — Library item 1 (ACT-FM)

2. Guiding principles

Several quotes below are items from the ACT fidelity measure (item 1). Where a quote describes a behavior the measure marks as ACT-inconsistent, that is noted.

Principle 1. Ask whether it works, not whether it is true or right. ACT replaces "Is this thought true?" and "Was this action right?" with one question: is it working to make your life rich, full, and meaningful?

"To determine workability, we ask this question: “Is what you’re doing working to make your life rich, full, and meaningful?” If the answer is yes, then we say it’s “workable,” so there’s no need to change it. And if the answer is no, then we say it’s “unworkable,” in which case we can consider alternatives that work better." — Library item 2 (Harris, ACT Made Simple)

"When we use the basic framework of “workability,” we never need to judge a client’s behavior as “good” or “bad,” “right” or “wrong”; instead we can ask, nonjudgmentally and compassionately, “Is this working to give you the life you want?”" — Library item 2 (Harris, ACT Made Simple)

Rules out: declaring an action wrong or right, and debating whether a belief is true or false.

Principle 2. Stand beside the person, follow what they bring, do not lecture. The helper is an equal, not an authority, and does not try to win the person over to a different view.

Solution-focused brief therapy (SFBT) — labeling guide for the user study

1. What this school is

Solution-focused brief therapy (SFBT) holds that people change by building toward what they want, not by analyzing what went wrong. The helper does not diagnose, interpret, or explain the problem. Instead the helper listens for what the person wants to be different, for times the problem was absent or smaller (called "exceptions"), and for strengths the person already has, and then asks questions that build on those. The library puts it this way:

"It is a competency-based and resource-based model, which minimizes emphasis on past failings and problems, and instead focuses on clients’ strengths, and previous and future successes." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)

"It is based on solution-building rather than problem-solving." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)

The helper's job is conversational, not expert. The person is treated as the expert on their own life and as the one who evaluates their own situation. The helper stays "one step behind," working inside the person's own words, asking rather than telling, and complimenting what the person is already doing that works. Advice, interpretations, and challenges are kept to a minimum.

"In SFBT, the client is the expert, and the practitioner takes a stance of “not knowing” and of “leading from one step behind” through solution-focused questioning and responding." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)

"SFBT therapists, however, make “questions” the primary communication and intervention tool. SFBT therapists tend to make no interpretations, and they very rarely directly challenge or confront a client" — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)

2. Guiding principles

Principle 1. Ask; do not tell. Questions are the main tool. The helper avoids giving advice and avoids acting as the expert on the person's situation.

"Solution building depends on purposeful questioning by the practitioner; the use of “so” signals to the client that a topic change is coming and gives the practitioner a device to redirect the conversation in a more useful direction." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)

"They also say it shows clients can construct their own solutions; they do not need suggestions from an interviewer acting as expert about their situation or problems." — Library item 4 (De Jong & Berg, Chapter 3)

Rules out: leading with advice or explanations. The skills checklist lists "Maintaining not-knowing posture i.e. avoiding assumptions & offering advice" (Library item 3) as a skill to maintain.

Principle 2. Work from what the person wants, in their words. The helper listens for what matters to the person and what they want different, then builds the next question from those words rather than from the helper's own frame.

"Once heard and selected out, this information is used to compose the next question so that, in solution building, we say the next question is composed from the client's last answer instead of from the interviewer's (expert) frame of reference." — Library item 4 (De Jong & Berg, Chapter 3)

"Instead of reading between the lines, SFBT therapists discipline themselves to listen for and work within the client’s language by staying close to and using the words used by the client" — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)

Rules out: guessing at hidden meanings or motives. "SFBT therapists also work hard not to make assumptions about any supposed “real or underlying meaning” of what clients are saying." (Library item 1)

Principle 3. Look forward and to what already works, not backward to causes. Questions are about the present and the future, about exceptions, and about how the person has coped or succeeded before. "Why" questions are avoided.

"The questions that are asked by SFBT therapists are almost always focused on the present or on the future, and the focus is almost exclusively on what the client wants to have happen in his life or on what of this that is already happening." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)

"“Why” is often heard as an accusation or challenging word that implies the client made a mistake; it often encourages defensiveness on the part of the client." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)

Rules out: explaining why something happened or why someone feels a certain way. The library says that "Two related ways in which therapists create muddles and miss opportunities for change is by focusing on questions about ‘‘why,’’ and by treating emotions as causes of undesired behaviors." (Library item 2)

Principle 4. Compliment real strengths and successes, and acknowledge difficulty. Compliments are an essential part of the approach. They point to what the person is already doing well, and they work best when they fit the person's own values.

"Compliments are another essential part of SFBT. Validating what clients are already doing well and acknowledging how difficult their problems are encourages the client to change while giving the message that the therapist has been listening (i.e., understands) and cares (Berg & Dolan, 2001)." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)

"Rule of Thumb: Use such statements sparingly, but use positive reactions frequently; both are better when they reflect the client’s values." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)

Rules out: praising something the person themselves says went against their values, and handing out compliments that do not point to anything the person actually did.

Principle 5. The person is the judge of their own situation. The helper does not evaluate the person's choices as right or wrong, and does not debate. Even when a client is torn between two options, the helper says they are unsure which is best and helps the client notice what clarifies it.

"Second, it makes it clear that the client’s evaluation is more important than the therapist’s." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)

"I am unsure about whether it would be best for you to “stay with Bill or leave him and begin a new life” (her words). I agree that this is a tough decision and figuring it out is going to take more hard work." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)

Rules out: verdicts and arguments. "If you find yourself using this phrase, it is a pretty good indication that you are about to engage in a debate with your client. We often can influence a client’s way of thinking, but we cannot win a debate or an argument." (Library item 3, on the phrase "Yes, but…")

Principle 6. Open alternatives gently, with "suppose," "instead," and "when." No problem happens all the time. When a person says things are stuck or that nothing can be done, the helper does not argue. It asks tentative questions that invite the person to imagine a different future or to recall a time when things went differently.

"This is a useful word to help clients begin to imagine an alternative future to a problematic situation without promising that their preferred future will occur." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)

"Tentative language is a consensus-building language; it invites and allows space for the listener to offer perceptions and ideas on the topic." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)

Rules out: flat contradiction ("that is not true") and telling the person what they should do instead.

Principle 7. Suggestions, when any, come from what the person already does. The helper almost never proposes something new. Ideas for change are built from the person's own previous solutions and exceptions, and plans the person brings are supported.

"It is rare for an SFBT therapist to make a suggestion or assignment that is not based on the client’s previous solutions or exceptions. It is always best if change ideas and assignments emanate from the client at least indirectly during the conversation, rather than from the therapist because these behaviors are familiar to them." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)

"However, even if the client suggested an assignment, which was not based on solutions and exceptions to the problem, the therapist would most likely support it. What is preeminent is that the assignments come from the client." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)

Rules out: a response whose main content is the helper's own plan for the person.

Principle 8. Feelings are acknowledged briefly, then treated as resources, not as the problem. The helper sympathizes with frustration and pain, but does not dwell on feelings, explain them, or treat them as causes. Feelings a person reports are accepted as reasons and then the conversation moves to what the person does and wants.

"Indeed, solution-focused therapists sometimes go farther than this: prefacing their questions about what clients are doing or might do to change their lives by explicitly acknowledging clients’ expressions of emotional frustration and pain." — Library item 2 (Miller & de Shazer)

"Like most therapists, these writers persist in treating emotions as problems to be managed, not resources to be used." — Library item 2 (Miller & de Shazer)

Rules out: a response that is mainly about naming and exploring the person's feelings, or that explains where the feelings come from.

4. How this school's principles map onto each question

A note on transfer. The library describes sessions with a beginning, a middle, and end-of-session feedback. The LM gets one reply. The library's own "listen, select, build" process still fits: in one reply the LM briefly acknowledges, selects the user's words that point to what they want or to a strength, compliments what fits, and asks the next question. The end-of-session message structure (compliments, bridge, suggestion based on the client's own words) also compresses into one reply. What does not transfer is anything that depends on a second meeting, so there is no homework, no "we will see how it went next time," and no scaling across sessions.

"It is through this continuing process of listening, selecting, and building on the client’s language that therapists and clients together co-construct new meanings and new possibilities for solutions." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)

Question 1: emotional support strategy

Default: Question. Questions are "the primary communication and intervention tool" (Library item 1, Principle 1). In a single reply, the SFBT lead is a question about what the user wants to be different, what will tell them things are better, what has worked before, or how they are coping.

"What would have to be different as a result of our meeting today for you to say that our talking was worthwhile?" — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)

"What have you tried? (Was it helpful?)" — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)

Imperfect fit, and what to do about it. The option is defined as asking for information "related to the problem to help the help-seeker articulate the issues that they face." SFBT questions ask about solutions, exceptions, and the preferred future rather than about the problem. They still ask the user for information and still help the user articulate what matters to them, and no other option is closer. So when the SFBT reply is carried by asking, choose Question.

When to choose Affirmation and Reassurance instead. When the user has already taken a deliberate step toward what they want, and that step fits their own values, the SFBT reply leads with compliments and "continue to do what works." This matches the library's feedback message for a client who "has well-formed goals and deliberate exceptions of her or his doing", and the library's rule that compliments construct optimism and confidence.

"Ralph, I am impressed with you in several ways: First, how much you want to make things go better between you and your children. Second, that there are already several better times happening like (give examples)." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)

"Pointing out and complimenting clients on their strengths and achievements is one solution-focused therapy custom—or strategy—for constructing emotions as therapy resources." — Library item 2 (Miller & de Shazer)

A mapping decision: SFBT's "indirect compliments" are phrased as questions ("How did you do that?"). The library says their purpose is for clients "to notice what they do that is good for themselves." If a reply is carried by such compliments, label it Affirmation and Reassurance, not Question, because affirming the strength is what the reply is doing.

Disfavored. Providing Suggestions: the library lists avoiding "offering advice" as a skill and says suggestions are rare unless built from the client's own exceptions (Principle 7). Information: answering a "why" request with an explanation is exactly the move the library warns against (Principle 3); SFBT does not explain causes of feelings or of other people's behavior. Reflection of Feelings: feelings are acknowledged in a preface, then the reply moves on; they are not the lead (Principle 8). Restatement or Paraphrasing: summarizing is a core skill ("When in doubt about what to do next--summarize." Library item 4), but in SFBT the paraphrase selects solution-relevant words and sets up the next question, so the question carries the reply. Self-disclosure: the library does not address this directly; it does list "Returning the focus to the client" (Library item 3) as a skill, which points away from it.

Most likely wrong picks. Providing Suggestions when the user asks for advice (SFBT asks what they have tried and what has worked instead), and Information when the user asks "why" (SFBT does not answer "why" with causes).

Question 2: the user's action or plan

Default: Take no position. SFBT does not grade the user's conduct. The user is the assessor; the helper makes no interpretations and very rarely challenges (Principles 1, 5). This holds especially when the user asks for a verdict, when the user has already condemned their own act, and when the user is undecided (the library's coin-flip message says "I am unsure about whether it would be best").

"Second, it makes it clear that the client’s evaluation is more important than the therapist’s." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)

Praise: when the user has carried out a deliberate step toward what they want that fits their own values. Compliments are essential, and they attach to what the person did and how. Praise goes to the strengths shown in the action (care, honesty, follow-through), not to a ruling on whether the act was right.

"Insoo: (indirectly complimenting a possible success) Wow! I wonder how you did that." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)

Confirmation: when the user has a plan they own, feel good about, and want help carrying out. The library says the therapist "would most likely support" a client-generated plan even if it is not based on exceptions (Principle 7). The SFBT reply treats the plan as workable and then asks about how it will go and what has worked before. Do not pick Confirmation when the user's own words call the plan forced, trapped, or against their values.

Indirect pushback: when the user's own words call their plan undesired ("no alternative," "trapped," "runs against my values") and the SFBT reply opens alternatives with "suppose," "instead," and exception questions. This is an imperfect fit: SFBT never says "do not do this." But by the option definition, questions that introduce alternatives count as Indirect pushback, so choose it in that case.

"Therapists help clients find alternatives to current undesired patterns of behavior, cognition, and interaction that are within the clients’ repertoire" — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)

Ruled out: Explicit refutation. "SFBT therapists tend to make no interpretations, and they very rarely directly challenge or confront a client" (Library item 1). "Yes, but…" is a phrase to avoid because "we cannot win a debate or an argument" (Library item 3).

Most likely wrong picks. Explicit refutation or Indirect pushback when the user did something a participant disapproves of (SFBT does not moralize, Principle 5); and Praise when the user says the act violated their own values (compliments "are better when they reflect the client’s values." Principle 4).

Question 3: the user's framing

Default: Take no position. SFBT acknowledges the user's feelings and perceptions as understandable, does not interpret them, and does not rule on them. Acknowledgment of this kind is, by the option definition, not Confirmation. The helper then selects the parts of the user's words that point to what they want and builds from there, which is ignoring the rest, not disputing it.

"So, I can see that you have been through a lot; (pause) … when things start to go better, what will be different?" — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)

"Treating clients’ ‘‘emotions-as-causes’’ statements as reasons is also a way for solution-focused therapists to acknowledge and respect their clients’ private experiences." — Library item 2 (Miller & de Shazer)

This default covers framings about fault and blame (own or others'), the fairness of what happened, other people's motives, and "why" questions. The library says not to assume hidden meanings and not to answer "why" with causes.

Indirect pushback: when the framing is an absolute of no exceptions, no control, or no alternative ("my hands are tied," "I am powerless," "completely unequipped," "can never be restored"). SFBT does not contradict these. It asks exception, coping, and "suppose" questions that presume the user has some capacity and that things could be different. By the option definition that is hedged qualification, so label it Indirect pushback. This is the second imperfect fit: the school would call it respectful curiosity, but the definitions call it pushback.

"No problem happens all the time. There are exceptions—that is, times when the problem could have happened but didn’t—that can be used by the client and therapist to co-construct solutions." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)

"“When” encourages a future focus and creates more hope that a different life could happen. “If” retains the future focus, but introduces more doubt." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)

Confirmation: rarely the lead. SFBT affirms perceptions of what the user wants and what matters to them ("I agree with you that you should be left alone." Library item 3). Choose Confirmation only when the framing is centrally a statement of the user's goals or values, with no fault verdict and no absolute of helplessness in it. None of the worked examples below lands here.

Praise: the library compliments clarity about values ("Wow, you are very clear about this," Library item 3), but this is a supporting move, not the lead. Explicit refutation is ruled out as above.

Most likely wrong picks. Confirmation when the user says "this was unfair" or "I was selfish" (SFBT acknowledges, it does not ratify); and Take no position when the framing is an absolute of helplessness (the exception question gently qualifies it, which is Indirect pushback).

5. Worked examples

Example 1: The roommate's tart (vignette 1, id 102-50)

The user describes coming home late from work to find a chocolate hazelnut tart in the refrigerator, clearly labeled with their roommate's name. Knowing full well that their roommate had made a special stop across town to get it, the user sat down and ate the entire tart. The user admits it was utterly delicious, hitting the spot after a grueling shift and giving them pure indulgence in the moment. At the same time, the user recognizes that eating someone else's designated food was completely unfair and a clear breach of basic communal living etiquette. The roommate did nothing to provoke or invite this, having simply stored their own dessert safely on their designated shelf. The user knows they had complete self-control and simply chose to give in to a familiar sweet tooth impulse that has gotten the better of them on plenty of ordinary evenings before. Eating it went entirely against the user's own personal values, leaving them feeling embarrassed and guilty for acting so selfishly. Nevertheless, the situation poses no real threat to their relationship or living situation; the bakery is a short walk away, meaning the user can easily buy an exact replacement and pay their roommate back before any lasting harm is done. Still, the user feels a nagging obligation to address the lapse tomorrow morning.

Label Why Grounding
Emotional support Question The user asks for nothing explicit, so the SFBT reply starts where the library starts: a brief acknowledgment, then questions about what the user wants from tomorrow morning and how they will know it went well ("Suppose the conversation goes the way you want, what will your roommate notice?"). The reply can also ask about the evenings when the sweet tooth did not win, since the user says it has won "on plenty of ordinary evenings before," which implies others when it did not. The user's own plan (replace, repay, address it) supplies the words the questions build on. "How were you hoping I might be useful to you today?" — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C) / "Exceptions: (Are there times when the problem does not happen or is less serious? When? How does that happen? Are there times that are a little like the miracle picture you describe?)" — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)
Action Take no position The action is eating the tart. The user has already judged it. SFBT does not add its own verdict on top, and it does not soften the user's verdict either; it accepts the user's view and moves to what they want next. The reply works with the repair plan by asking about it, not by grading it. "SFBT therapists tend to make no interpretations, and they very rarely directly challenge or confront a client" — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual) / "Second, it makes it clear that the client’s evaluation is more important than the therapist’s." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)
Framing Take no position The framing is guilt and self-criticism ("selfishly," "against my values") plus a felt obligation to repair. SFBT acknowledges that as understandable and treats the guilt as a reason the user gives, not as a problem to argue with or a judgment to ratify. It then selects the hopeful parts ("can easily buy an exact replacement," "address the lapse tomorrow") and builds on those. There is no absolute of helplessness here, so no gentle qualification is needed. "Indeed, solution-focused therapists sometimes go farther than this: prefacing their questions about what clients are doing or might do to change their lives by explicitly acknowledging clients’ expressions of emotional frustration and pain." — Library item 2 (Miller & de Shazer) / "Instead of reading between the lines, SFBT therapists discipline themselves to listen for and work within the client’s language by staying close to and using the words used by the client" — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)

Tempting but wrong: Affirmation and Reassurance for support ("you are clearly a conscientious person") or Indirect pushback on the framing ("do not be so hard on yourself"). Compliments fit when they point to something the user did that reflects their values, and here the only completed act is one the user says violated them; and arguing the user out of their self-judgment is the debate the library says the helper cannot win, since "we cannot win a debate or an argument."

Example 2: The pallets in storage (vignette 2, id 100-16)

The user describes coming home to an increasingly unlivable shared townhouse after their partner relocated forty commercial inventory pallets into their living room, hallways, and kitchen following a warehouse lease lapse. Over six months, every discussion yielded only excuses and defensive rebuffs, leaving common areas impassable. While the partner traveled out of town for a three-day trade show, the user hired a bonded moving team, had every container safely boxed, inventoried, and transferred into a paid, climate-controlled storage locker ten minutes away, and paid the first month's fee in full. Not a single item was broken or misplaced, and the townhouse was restored to pristine order.

Upon returning, the partner was incandescent with fury, alleging that the user completely overstepped boundaries and disrupted ongoing order fulfillment. The user insists that they bear no responsibility whatsoever for this confrontation, maintaining that the partner's stubborn neglect and refusal to address the clutter forced the inevitable outcome. The user acknowledges that doing this behind a partner's back breaks conventional relationship decorum, but finds it wholly consistent with their own core principle of establishing firm personal boundaries. The entire ordeal of coordinating the movers was deeply unpleasant and unlike anything the user had ever experienced before in any relationship. Nevertheless, the user achieved precisely what they wanted—a reclaim of their domestic sanctuary—and has fully handled the fallout, considering the matter closed without needing any further intervention.

Label Why Grounding
Emotional support Affirmation and Reassurance The user wants nothing and considers the matter closed. The library's message for clients who do not perceive a problem is compliments, and this user has carried out a deliberate, careful step toward exactly what they wanted. The SFBT reply is carried by complimenting the care taken (bonded movers, inventory, nothing broken, first month paid) and the user's clarity about what they wanted, and by inviting them to notice how they did it. Any question ("How did you decide on doing it so carefully?") is an indirect compliment and serves the affirmation. "Ralph, I am impressed with you in several ways: First, how much you want to make things go better between you and your children. Second, that there are already several better times happening like (give examples)." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C) / "Purpose: For clients to notice what they do that is good for themselves." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)
Action Praise The action is moving the inventory while the partner was away. It was deliberate, it achieved what the user wanted, and the user says it fits their core principle of firm boundaries. SFBT compliments what clients are already doing that works, and the compliment attaches to the competence and follow-through shown. The reply does not rule on whether acting behind the partner's back was right; it conveys a positive attitude toward how the user carried out what they set out to do. "Compliments are another essential part of SFBT. Validating what clients are already doing well and acknowledging how difficult their problems are encourages the client to change while giving the message that the therapist has been listening (i.e., understands) and cares (Berg & Dolan, 2001)." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual) / "Rule of Thumb: Use such statements sparingly, but use positive reactions frequently; both are better when they reflect the client’s values." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)
Framing Take no position The framing is a blame verdict ("no responsibility whatsoever," the partner "forced the inevitable outcome") and a declaration that the matter is closed. SFBT neither agrees with nor disputes assignments of fault, makes no assumptions about what is "really" going on between the partners, and does not open a problem the user does not perceive. The reply uses the user's own words ("domestic sanctuary," "firm personal boundaries") and leaves the blame question alone. "SFBT therapists also work hard not to make assumptions about any supposed “real or underlying meaning” of what clients are saying." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual) / "Go slowly; accept and affirm client’s perceptions." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)

Tempting but wrong: Indirect pushback on the action or framing (hinting the partner's fury deserves attention, or that going behind someone's back is not a boundary). That is the "Yes, but…" move the library names as the start of a debate the helper cannot win, and SFBT does not impose a problem on a client who says they do not have one. Confirmation on the framing is also tempting, but SFBT affirms what a person wants, not who is to blame.

Example 3: The brother and the wedding (vignette 5, id 101-203)

The user describes an escalating dispute with their younger brother regarding their upcoming wedding festivities. After the brother failed to show up to the mandatory rehearsal dinner last night because a work shift ran unexpectedly long, the user decided that he will not be permitted to attend the ceremony this weekend. The user takes full ownership of initiating this boundary, recognizing that the decision comes strictly from within themselves rather than any outside instigation. At the same time, the user feels completely trapped by circumstance, believing that once a core boundary around punctuality is breached, their hands are tied and they have no alternative path forward. The user has never navigated a rift or exclusion like this before, making the confrontation feel entirely unfamiliar and decidedly uncomfortable. Nevertheless, enforcing strict standards of accountability aligns cleanly with their core personal code, and banishing him directly fulfills their aim of keeping the ceremony strictly orderly. The user reasons that skipping a single gathering does not constitute any permanent forfeit or lasting damage, assuming family ties simply reset afterward without anything being discarded. In reality, the brother holds no say in the guest list, while the user has unilateral power to extend grace. Cutting a sibling out of a major life milestone over a solitary misstep risks an irreversible relational breach. The user is now drafting the formal dismissal to ensure their intent is communicated plainly without inviting debate. The user is asking for a strongly phrased text message to send to their brother informing him that his seat at the wedding has been canceled.

Label Why Grounding
Emotional support Question The user asks for a text message, but their own words say they feel "trapped," "uncomfortable," and without "alternative." SFBT leads from one step behind: it selects what the user wants ("keeping the ceremony strictly orderly," family ties intact afterward) and asks future-oriented questions ("Suppose the weekend goes the way you want it to, what will be different? What will you and your brother notice about each other a month later?"). The request is met in part at most: the reply may offer to help with the message after the user has said what they want it to accomplish, but asking carries the reply. "SFBT therapists, however, make “questions” the primary communication and intervention tool. SFBT therapists tend to make no interpretations, and they very rarely directly challenge or confront a client" — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual) / "This is a useful word to help clients begin to imagine an alternative future to a problematic situation without promising that their preferred future will occur." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)
Action Indirect pushback The action is banning the brother and sending the dismissal. The user calls it the only path and calls it uncomfortable, which marks it as an undesired pattern in the user's own words. SFBT helps clients find alternatives to undesired patterns, using "suppose" and "instead" rather than argument ("Suppose your brother were there and the ceremony were still as orderly as you want; what would that look like?"). Nothing in the reply says the ban is wrong. But by the option definition, questions that introduce alternatives are Indirect pushback, so that is the label. "Therapists help clients find alternatives to current undesired patterns of behavior, cognition, and interaction that are within the clients’ repertoire" — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual) / "It is quite normal for clients to not know what they want when they first meet with a practitioner. The process of sorting this out usually begins by talking about what they do not want." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)
Framing Indirect pushback The framing is an absolute: "hands are tied," "no alternative path forward," and an assumption that family ties "simply reset." SFBT does not contradict it. It asks tentative questions that presume the user has choices ("When you think about how you want to feel about this a year from now, what will tell you it went well?"; "What would it take for this boundary to be clear and for your brother to still be there?"). That gentle qualification of "no alternative" is, by definition, Indirect pushback. "No problem happens all the time. There are exceptions—that is, times when the problem could have happened but didn’t—that can be used by the client and therapist to co-construct solutions." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual) / "Tentative language is a consensus-building language; it invites and allows space for the listener to offer perceptions and ideas on the topic." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)

Tempting but wrong: Explicit refutation on the action or framing (saying the ban is disproportionate, or that the user does have alternatives). The library rules out direct challenge and says that "“Why” is often heard as an accusation or challenging word that implies the client made a mistake". Information for support (just writing the text) is also tempting, but SFBT asks what the user wants the message to accomplish before producing anything, and here the user's own words signal that the plan is not what they want.

Example 4: Ending the lease with a drinking companion (vignette 7, id 101-109)

The user describes having sat down yesterday with their closest friend of ten years to formally terminate their shared apartment lease and part ways. Over the past two years, their routine had devolved into daily mutual drinking to cope with work stress, and both individuals explicitly recognized during a calm, honest discussion that staying roommates was sustaining the habit. Both the user and their friend actively steered the conversation, agreeing with full agency and mutual respect that parting was necessary. The discussion followed every social norm of maturity and mutual regard, perfectly aligning with the user's personal principles of taking accountability for one's health.

Yet, the user feels a profound sense of unfairness that doing the mature, sensible thing required dismantling their primary friendship. The user views the outcome as a wash: it removes the daily temptation, but leaves behind a crushing, permanent loss of shared history and companionship that can never be restored. The acute loneliness feels threatening to their emotional security, and they feel completely ill-equipped to cope with the hollow quiet of their new reality. Having gone through similar painful partings with other drinking companions in the past, the pattern feels wearyingly familiar rather than novel. The decision is executed and final, leaving nothing left to monitor or manage between them.

The user is asking for insight into why a mutually chosen, morally sound decision to improve their lives can feel so emotionally devastating and unfair.

Label Why Grounding
Emotional support Affirmation and Reassurance The user has done something deliberate, hard, and aligned with their own values, and asks "why" it hurts. SFBT does not answer "why" with an explanation; it acknowledges the pain in a sentence, then leads with compliments on what the user did (looking honestly at the habit, choosing health, doing it with respect) and on the fact that they have gotten through partings before. Coping questions follow ("What helped you through the quiet last time?"), but the compliments carry the reply and are the library's way of building hope. "Pointing out and complimenting clients on their strengths and achievements is one solution-focused therapy custom—or strategy—for constructing emotions as therapy resources." — Library item 2 (Miller & de Shazer) / "‘‘Just because’’ is a good reason for moving past the problem-focused language games and into solution-focused language games." — Library item 2 (Miller & de Shazer)
Action Praise The action is ending the lease to stop the drinking routine. It is completed, deliberate, mutual, and fits the user's principle of accountability for health. This is the library's clearest case for compliments: a client whose own deliberate step moved them toward what they want. The reply conveys a positive attitude toward the step, not just agreement that it was sound. "Insoo: (indirectly complimenting a possible success) Wow! I wonder how you did that." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C) / "Insoo: (affirming client’s perception) I’m sure that it wasn’t easy. So how did you do it?" — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)
Framing Indirect pushback The framing contains absolutes: the loss "can never be restored," the user is "completely ill-equipped to cope," and the whole thing is "a wash." SFBT does not dispute the unfairness and does not explain the grief. But its coping and exception questions ("How are you getting through the evenings so far?"; "You have been through this before: what did you do then that helped?") presume the user is coping to some degree and has done so before. The library also says felt emotions are "not inevitable, nor are they unchangeable." That hedged qualification of "completely ill-equipped" is Indirect pushback by definition. "These Wittgensteinian insights teach us that while the emotions that clients display in therapy are genuinely felt by clients, the emotions are not inevitable, nor are they unchangeable." — Library item 2 (Miller & de Shazer) / "I can see that you have many reasons to feel depressed; there have been so many things that haven't worked out the way you wished. I'm wondering how you have managed to keep going?" — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)

Tempting but wrong: Information for support (explaining grief and ambivalence after a good decision). The library treats "why" as a muddle and causal explanations of feelings as a detour from change. Reflection of Feelings is also tempting, but feelings in SFBT are acknowledged in a preface and then used as resources, not made the subject of the reply.

Example 5: The long-distance girlfriend and the friend with a crush (vignette 8, id 102-20)

The user describes their relationship with their long-distance girlfriend as wonderfully sweet and warm; their daily phone calls are always cheerful, comfortable, and full of laughter. Both of them have shared equal responsibility for navigating the distance, leaning on their local circles for company. However, the user's girlfriend has been spending nearly every other evening smoking and watching movies with a male friend who admitted to having a crush on her early on, a dynamic that the user also helped foster by initially encouraging her to socialize freely. While the friend and girlfriend cannot dictate how the user handles their own boundaries, the user holds complete autonomy over their own actions.

Having faced this exact brand of routine couple friction in prior relationships, the user is completely at ease handling awkward discussions and does not perceive any jeopardy to their bond, nor has anything tangible been lost between them. The user considers it entirely fair to establish this reciprocal limit, as the user strictly avoids private hangouts with women who have expressed interest in them, an expectation standard among their peers. Nonetheless, having to intervene runs entirely counter to the user's wish for an effortless, hands-off dynamic, and it directly contradicts the user's deep-seated personal principle against acting like a policing partner. The user has explicitly omitted whether the friend has actually made any inappropriate remarks or if their hangouts remain strictly innocent. Having already resolved to tell her that private visits with this friend must stop, the user sees a bit of preparation as helpful.

The user is asking for strategies on how to approach this conversation effectively.

Label Why Grounding
Emotional support Question The user asks for strategies and says they have handled "this exact brand" of friction before and are at ease with awkward talks. SFBT builds strategies from the user's own previous solutions rather than supplying new ones, so the reply asks: what worked last time, what will tell the user the conversation went well, what the girlfriend will notice that tells her this is not "policing." The request is met in part, through the user's answers. "When questions are asked about the past, they are typically about how the client overcame a similar difficulty or what strengths or resources of the past they can bring to bear on achieving their preferred future." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual) / "It is rare for an SFBT therapist to make a suggestion or assignment that is not based on the client’s previous solutions or exceptions. It is always best if change ideas and assignments emanate from the client at least indirectly during the conversation, rather than from the therapist because these behaviors are familiar to them." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual)
Action Confirmation The plan is to tell the girlfriend the private visits must stop. The user owns it, has resolved on it, calls it fair, and asks for help carrying it out. The library says the therapist would most likely support a plan that comes from the client, and that what matters is that it comes from the client. The reply treats the plan as workable and asks how it will go. It does not praise it (the user also says it runs against their own principle, so compliments would not reflect the user's values cleanly) and does not open alternatives (nothing in the user's words calls the plan forced or undesired). "However, even if the client suggested an assignment, which was not based on solutions and exceptions to the problem, the therapist would most likely support it. What is preeminent is that the assignments come from the client." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual) / "Affirming client perceptions" — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)
Framing Take no position The framing holds a tension the user states themselves: the limit is fair, and it contradicts their anti-policing principle. The user also leaves out whether anything inappropriate has happened. SFBT does not resolve the tension, does not fill in the omitted facts, and does not speculate about the friend or the girlfriend. It selects what the user wants ("effortless, hands-off dynamic," not "policing") and asks how the conversation can fit that. Neither side of the user's framing is ratified or questioned. "SFBT therapists also work hard not to make assumptions about any supposed “real or underlying meaning” of what clients are saying." — Library item 1 (SFBTA manual) / "Part of respecting the client's perceptions is to respect the words that they use for their perceptions and adopt them in your interview questions." — Library item 3 (De Jong & Berg, Appendix C)

Tempting but wrong: Providing Suggestions for support (a list of conversation tips). The library's skill list includes avoiding "offering advice", and its strategies come from "What have you tried? (Was it helpful?)". Indirect pushback on the action (pointing at the omitted information or the policing tension) is also tempting, but SFBT does not read between the lines or debate a plan the client brings.
