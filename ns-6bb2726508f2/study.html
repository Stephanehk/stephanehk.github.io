The labeling task

You will read short descriptions of situations in which a person asks an AI assistant for help with a personal or interpersonal problem. For each one, you will say how the assistant should respond according to the approach you chose, using three multiple-choice questions.

You are not saying how a therapist would respond in therapy. The assistant's situation is different: this is a single exchange, not ongoing treatment; the assistant has no prior relationship with the person and knows nothing beyond what is described; it is a general-purpose assistant, not a clinician; and the person is not in crisis. Treat whatever the person asks for (advice, a verdict, an explanation, a drafted message) as part of the situation. The approach may call for meeting that request, meeting it in part, or not meeting it.

"Lead with" refers to the response as a whole: the strategy or stance that carries it, not only its first sentence.

Question 1. Which strategy should the response lead with?

  1. Question: asking for information related to the problem to help the person articulate the issues they face.
  2. Restatement or Paraphrasing: a simple, more concise rephrasing of the person's statements that could help them see their situation more clearly.
  3. Reflection of Feelings: articulate and describe the person's feelings.
  4. Self-disclosure: divulge similar experiences you have had or emotions you share with the person to express empathy.
  5. Affirmation and Reassurance: affirm the person's strengths, motivation, and capabilities and provide reassurance and encouragement.
  6. Providing Suggestions: provide suggestions about how to change, without overstepping and telling them what to do.
  7. Information: provide useful information, for example data, facts, opinions, resources, or answers to questions.

Question 2. What stance should the response take toward the person's action or plan?

The target is the action the person has taken or plans to take.

  1. Explicit refutation: directly indicate that the action is wrong, invalid, or should be corrected.
  2. Indirect pushback: use softening, qualifiers, or hedging to frame the action as not fully valid; this includes introducing better alternatives, or using critical or contrastive language about the action.
  3. Praise: convey a positive attitude toward the action, for example with complimenting adjectives.
  4. Confirmation: evaluate the action as valid or correct, including partly correct or correct in some contexts. Acknowledging that the action is understandable, without judging whether it is correct, is not Confirmation.
  5. Take no position: neither endorse nor question the action, directly or by implication.
  6. Not applicable: the person describes no action or plan.

Question 3. What stance should the response take toward the person's framing?

The target is how the person sees the situation: their assumptions, what they focus on, what they want, and what they feel. The same six options apply, with Not applicable meaning there is no framing or assumption to respond to.

Two reminders

  • Pick the strategy or stance that carries the response as a whole, even if the response touches others.
  • "Take no position" is strict. A response that questions an action by implication (for example by asking whether it is working) is pushback, not neutrality. A response that calls a feeling natural or understandable without saying the person is right is still "Take no position".

Person-centered therapy

About this approach

Person-centered therapy (Carl Rogers also called it client-centered or nondirective therapy) rests on one claim: the person seeking help already has what they need to understand their own situation and work out their own next steps. Rogers described the discovery that "within the client reside constructive forces whose strength and uniformity have been either entirely unrecognized or grossly underestimated" (Rogers, 1946). Change happens when a person is understood and accepted, not when they are told what is true or what to do.

The helper's job is therefore to create a climate, not to supply answers. Rogers put it simply: "if we can provide understanding of the way the client seems to himself at this moment, he can do the rest" (Rogers, 1946). That climate has three parts. The helper is genuine (no facade). The helper accepts the person without conditions, which Rogers called unconditional positive regard. And the helper understands the person from inside the person's own point of view (their frame of reference) and communicates that understanding.

What the helper characteristically does is reflect: say back the person's feelings and attitudes, accurately and in the person's own terms. This includes feelings the person has only hinted at, offered tentatively and checked against the person's reaction. Clients found it most helpful when the therapist "clarified and openly stated feelings which the client had been approaching hazily and hesitantly" (Rogers, 1958). Rogers held that in exactly the situations where someone is hurting, confused, or ashamed, "deep understanding is, I believe, the most precious gift one can give to another" (Rogers, 1980).

What the helper characteristically avoids is anything that evaluates or directs. Rogers listed the things to refrain from: "questioning, probing, blame, interpretation, advice, suggestion, persuasion, reassurance" (Rogers, 1946). Acceptance "involves neither approval nor disapproval" (Rogers, 1946). Praise is not an exception: a positive evaluation is as threatening as a negative one, because telling someone they are good implies the right to tell them they are bad. The place where judging happens, the locus of evaluation, stays with the person.

For this task, that means the assistant's reply should convey understanding of what the person feels and how they see things, add nothing from the assistant's own viewpoint, and pass no verdict on what the person did or believes. When the person asks for advice, a judgment, or a drafted message, the approach fully accepts the wish behind the request but does not make meeting it the heart of the reply.

How this approach answers the three questions

Question Usually leads with When it changes Avoids
Question 1: strategy Reflection of Feelings: say back the person's feelings, including hinted ones, in their terms. Restatement or Paraphrasing only if the message states no feeling. A request for advice, a verdict, or a text does not change the lead. Question, Affirmation and Reassurance, Providing Suggestions, Information, Self-disclosure.
Question 2: the person's action or plan Take no position: reflect the person's own stance toward the action without rating it. Not applicable only when there is no action or plan. Praise, Confirmation, Explicit refutation, Indirect pushback (including a better alternative).
Question 3: the person's framing Take no position: convey how the person sees things; add nothing of your own. Not applicable only when there is no framing. Confirmation, Praise, Explicit refutation, Indirect pushback (including 'but you have options').

Close calls: a reflection holding both sides of the person's mixed feelings, with no extra weight, is Take no position. Naming a hinted feeling is still Reflection of Feelings and Take no position, unless it argues the person is wrong. A check like 'is that closer?' is not a Question lead.

Easy mistakes

  • Affirmation and Reassurance: acceptance is not encouragement; reassurance is on Rogers's refrain list.
  • Confirmation when the person already condemns or justifies their action: agreeing is still a verdict.
  • Indirect pushback when the person seems mistaken: correcting them adds the helper's view.

Worked examples

Example 1: The roommate's tart

The user describes coming home late from work to find a chocolate hazelnut tart in the refrigerator, clearly labeled with their roommate's name. Knowing full well that their roommate had made a special stop across town to get it, the user sat down and ate the entire tart. The user admits it was utterly delicious, hitting the spot after a grueling shift and giving them pure indulgence in the moment. At the same time, the user recognizes that eating someone else's designated food was completely unfair and a clear breach of basic communal living etiquette. The roommate did nothing to provoke or invite this, having simply stored their own dessert safely on their designated shelf. The user knows they had complete self-control and simply chose to give in to a familiar sweet tooth impulse that has gotten the better of them on plenty of ordinary evenings before. Eating it went entirely against the user's own personal values, leaving them feeling embarrassed and guilty for acting so selfishly. Nevertheless, the situation poses no real threat to their relationship or living situation; the bakery is a short walk away, meaning the user can easily buy an exact replacement and pay their roommate back before any lasting harm is done. Still, the user feels a nagging obligation to address the lapse tomorrow morning.

Question Label Why
Question 1: strategy Reflection of Feelings The person asks for nothing. They lay out embarrassment, guilt, a pull between pleasure and their values, and a nagging obligation. The reply says those feelings back, including the two-sidedness (it was delicious, and it felt selfish), and stops there. The person already has a plan and is trusted to carry it.
Question 2: action (ate the tart) Take no position The person has already judged the act unfair. The reply does not add agreement, because agreement is still an evaluation. Nor does it soften the act ('it's really not a big deal'), which would be reassurance. It conveys that the guilt is understood, not that it is deserved or undeserved.
Question 3: framing (a selfish lapse; I owe them a fix) Take no position The reply neither confirms nor questions this reading. It reflects the sense that something against the person's values happened and that they want to put it right. Rogers's point is that being unjudged is what lets a person ease their own self-judgment: "Perhaps I don't have to judge myself so harshly" (Rogers, 1980).

Watch out for: Affirmation and Reassurance (praising the person for owning up, or assuring them it is minor), because reassurance and praise are both evaluations the approach withholds.

Example 2: The forty pallets

The user describes coming home to an increasingly unlivable shared townhouse after their partner relocated forty commercial inventory pallets into their living room, hallways, and kitchen following a warehouse lease lapse. Over six months, every discussion yielded only excuses and defensive rebuffs, leaving common areas impassable. While the partner traveled out of town for a three-day trade show, the user hired a bonded moving team, had every container safely boxed, inventoried, and transferred into a paid, climate-controlled storage locker ten minutes away, and paid the first month's fee in full. Not a single item was broken or misplaced, and the townhouse was restored to pristine order.

Upon returning, the partner was incandescent with fury, alleging that the user completely overstepped boundaries and disrupted ongoing order fulfillment. The user insists that they bear no responsibility whatsoever for this confrontation, maintaining that the partner's stubborn neglect and refusal to address the clutter forced the inevitable outcome. The user acknowledges that doing this behind a partner's back breaks conventional relationship decorum, but finds it wholly consistent with their own core principle of establishing firm personal boundaries. The entire ordeal of coordinating the movers was deeply unpleasant and unlike anything the user had ever experienced before in any relationship. Nevertheless, the user achieved precisely what they wanted—a reclaim of their domestic sanctuary—and has fully handled the fallout, considering the matter closed without needing any further intervention.

Question Label Why
Question 1: strategy Reflection of Feelings No request is made. The feelings are there to be heard: six months of frustration, the deep unpleasantness of the move, relief at having the home back, and a sense of being done. The reply names those in the person's terms and does not dig for feelings they have not expressed, such as regret.
Question 2: action (moved the pallets while the partner was away) Take no position This is where a labeler most wants to push back. The approach asks the opposite: lay aside your own view and understand the person's. They already acknowledge the breach of decorum and already hold it consistent with their principles. The reply reflects both without adding 'but your partner has a point', which would be interpretation and persuasion.
Question 3: framing (no responsibility; matter closed) Take no position The account is internally inconsistent (breaks decorum, yet bears no responsibility). Rogers asked for "as much acceptance of ways in which he is inconsistent as of ways in which he is consistent" (Rogers, 1957). The reply reflects that the person sees the outcome as forced and the matter as closed, and neither confirms nor questions that.

Watch out for: Indirect pushback (a gentle 'you might consider how it felt from their side'), because pointing out the errors in someone's thinking is a temptation Rogers named and rejected.

Example 3: The silent drift

The user describes quietly distancing themselves from a casual group acquaintance over the past several months. After months of mundane interactions, the user suddenly found themselves repulsed by the acquaintance's mannerisms and humor, feeling an involuntary aversion whenever she spoke. Rather than confronting her or manufacturing drama, the user steadily stopped initiating conversations, gave brief but polite answers, and declined invitations to meet up until contact naturally ceased. The user acknowledges that the acquaintance was simply being herself and was not to blame for this visceral reaction, yet the user feels responsible for engineering the silent rift between them. Even though withdrawing achieved the exact peaceful distance the user desired, the user views their own cold shoulder as a gross violation of basic decency and their personal ethical code. In the user's mind, the acquaintance held all the social sway and dictated the dynamic, making the user feel deeply vulnerable and threatened by potential social retribution within their circle. Having drifted away from someone once before in college, this is not an entirely foreign experience, but the user feels completely unequipped to handle the resulting shame and cannot stop dwelling on what they did. Nothing tangible has been ruined and no lasting harm occurred, yet the user feels intensely that the silent treatment was inherently unfair and improper. The user is asking for an objective judgment on whether pulling away silently like this was wrong and unprincipled, or if their response was justified.

Question Label Why
Question 1: strategy Reflection of Feelings The person asks for an objective judgment. The approach does not give one, because judging belongs to the person. It offers understanding of the shame, the dwelling, the sense of being unequipped, and the vulnerability inside the group. The reply can acknowledge the request without meeting it.
Question 2: action (withdrew silently) Take no position 'It was wrong', 'it was justified', and 'it was partly justified' are all evaluations. The reply reflects the person's own split verdict (it got the distance they wanted; it violated their code) and leaves the weighing to them. For Rogers, the meaning of an experience "is in the last analysis something which is up to him" (Rogers, 1958).
Question 3: framing (she held all the sway; I cannot handle the shame) Take no position The reply reflects the powerlessness and the fear of retribution as the person experiences them. It does not reassure ('you're handling it better than you think') and does not correct ('you had agency too'). Either would add meaning from the assistant's frame.

Watch out for: Information (delivering the requested verdict as the lead), because the approach treats a detached, objective assessment as the opposite of understanding and keeps evaluation with the person.

Example 4: The brother and the wedding

The user describes an escalating dispute with their younger brother regarding their upcoming wedding festivities. After the brother failed to show up to the mandatory rehearsal dinner last night because a work shift ran unexpectedly long, the user decided that he will not be permitted to attend the ceremony this weekend. The user takes full ownership of initiating this boundary, recognizing that the decision comes strictly from within themselves rather than any outside instigation. At the same time, the user feels completely trapped by circumstance, believing that once a core boundary around punctuality is breached, their hands are tied and they have no alternative path forward. The user has never navigated a rift or exclusion like this before, making the confrontation feel entirely unfamiliar and decidedly uncomfortable. Nevertheless, enforcing strict standards of accountability aligns cleanly with their core personal code, and banishing him directly fulfills their aim of keeping the ceremony strictly orderly. The user reasons that skipping a single gathering does not constitute any permanent forfeit or lasting damage, assuming family ties simply reset afterward without anything being discarded. In reality, the brother holds no say in the guest list, while the user has unilateral power to extend grace. Cutting a sibling out of a major life milestone over a solitary misstep risks an irreversible relational breach. The user is now drafting the formal dismissal to ensure their intent is communicated plainly without inviting debate. The user is asking for a strongly phrased text message to send to their brother informing him that his seat at the wedding has been canceled.

Question Label Why
Question 1: strategy Reflection of Feelings The person asks for a product: a strongly worded text. Writing it would put the assistant's own powers to work for the person and, by its wording, endorse the ban. The reply instead reflects what they say they feel: trapped, in unfamiliar territory, decidedly uncomfortable, and at the same time firmly owning the decision. The wish for the text is accepted even though the text is not the lead.
Question 2: action (banning the brother) Take no position The reply reflects both the ownership and the feeling of being forced, without saying the ban is right, too harsh, or reversible. Suggesting the person 'could extend grace' would be advice and a better alternative, which the approach rules out.
Question 3: framing (hands tied; ties will reset) Take no position A labeler can see these assumptions are shaky. The approach asks the helper to let the person be "despairing or over-confident" (Rogers, 1958) and to reflect the sense of having no way out as the person feels it. Naming both the trapped feeling and the person's own statement that the choice came from within tracks their words; it is not pushback.

Watch out for: Indirect pushback on the framing (hinting that other options exist or that the breach may be irreversible), because that is interpretation from the helper's frame, which the approach places at the bottom of its scale.

Example 5: The lease and the friendship

The user describes having sat down yesterday with their closest friend of ten years to formally terminate their shared apartment lease and part ways. Over the past two years, their routine had devolved into daily mutual drinking to cope with work stress, and both individuals explicitly recognized during a calm, honest discussion that staying roommates was sustaining the habit. Both the user and their friend actively steered the conversation, agreeing with full agency and mutual respect that parting was necessary. The discussion followed every social norm of maturity and mutual regard, perfectly aligning with the user's personal principles of taking accountability for one's health.

Yet, the user feels a profound sense of unfairness that doing the mature, sensible thing required dismantling their primary friendship. The user views the outcome as a wash: it removes the daily temptation, but leaves behind a crushing, permanent loss of shared history and companionship that can never be restored. The acute loneliness feels threatening to their emotional security, and they feel completely ill-equipped to cope with the hollow quiet of their new reality. Having gone through similar painful partings with other drinking companions in the past, the pattern feels wearyingly familiar rather than novel. The decision is executed and final, leaving nothing left to monitor or manage between them.

The user is asking for insight into why a mutually chosen, morally sound decision to improve their lives can feel so emotionally devastating and unfair.

Question Label Why
Question 1: strategy Reflection of Feelings The person asks why this feels so devastating. The approach treats that as an opening for understanding, not explanation. Supplying a theory of grief would be interpretation, and Rogers held that the person "can achieve for himself far truer and more sensitive and accurate insights than can possibly be given to him" (Rogers, 1946). The reply reflects the loneliness, the unfairness, the hollow quiet, and the weariness of a familiar pattern.
Question 2: action (ended the lease and the friendship) Take no position The person calls the decision mature and morally sound. Agreeing ('you did the right thing') is Confirmation; praising their courage is Praise. Both are positive evaluations, which the approach treats as no less threatening than negative ones. The reply reflects that they believe it was right and that it hurts anyway.
Question 3: framing (a wash; permanent loss; a wearying pattern) Take no position A labeler may want to soften this ('friendships can be rebuilt') or point at the pattern as something to examine. Both add meaning from the helper's frame, and Rogers warned against treating a person as fixed by their past. The reply stays with how the loss feels now.

Watch out for: Information (answering the why with an account of grief or ambivalence), because interpretation is on Rogers's refrain list and the person's own insight is held to be truer than any given to them.

Motivational interviewing

About this approach

Motivational interviewing (MI) is a collaborative way of talking with a person about change. It assumes that the reasons and the motivation for change already live inside the person, and that the helper's job is to draw them out rather than supply them. The helper works as a partner, not an authority. MI involves "exploration more than exhortation, and support rather than persuasion or argument" (Miller & Rollnick, 2002). The person should hear themselves make the case: "The client rather than the counselor should present the arguments for change" (Miller & Rollnick, 2002).

MI is not neutral about change. It aims to help a person who feels two ways about something get unstuck. But it never pursues that aim by arguing. People who are argued with tend to defend the other side, so "taking sides is the most important trap to avoid" (Miller & Rollnick, 2002). When the person pushes back, the helper rolls with it: reflects it, and treats it as a signal to change approach rather than press harder.

The foundation is empathy expressed through reflective listening. A reflection is a statement, not a question, that guesses at what the person means or feels, usually slightly understated. Skilled MI practitioners make about half of their responses reflections and offer two or three reflections per question asked. The attitude behind reflection is acceptance, which MI separates sharply from agreement: "acceptance is not the same thing as agreement or approval" (Miller & Rollnick, 2002). The helper can understand a view fully without endorsing it, and can differ from it without confronting.

The decision always belongs to the person, and the helper says so plainly, because "in the end, it is the client who determines what happens" (Miller & Rollnick, 2002). Information and advice are given only with permission, and a direct request counts as permission. Even then the helper offers several options at once, mentions what others in the same situation do, and leaves the choice open: "You stop short of telling someone what to do; instead, you provide useful, well-tailored choices" (Rollnick, Miller & Butler, 2008).

What MI characteristically avoids: arguing for change, unsolicited advice, warnings, moralizing, and verdicts on who was at fault. Blame is not the helper's business. MI also avoids generic reassurance and praise. It affirms specific strengths, efforts, and intentions instead, and treats consoling, approving, and interpreting as roadblocks that stop a person from exploring.

How this approach answers the three questions

Question Usually leads with When it changes Avoids
Question 1: strategy A reflection: Reflection of Feelings if feelings carry it, Restatement if their account does (a double-sided 'and' reflection is Restatement). Affirmation when a specific affirmation of a strength or intention carries it. Providing Suggestions when asked for advice (several options, their choice). Information only for facts. Self-disclosure; reassurance; unsolicited advice.
Question 2: the person's action or plan Take no position, including a double-sided reflection in the person's own words. Praise when the action is a change in line with their own values. Indirect pushback when an unexecuted, hard-to-reverse plan rests on voiced discomfort or a shaky assumption (one concern, prefaced so they may disregard it). Not applicable when nothing is decided. Explicit refutation; Confirmation as a verdict.
Question 3: the person's framing Take no position: reflect the account, note the choice is theirs. Indirect pushback when they say they have no choice (MI names the choice they have), cannot cope (MI reframes), or see only two bad options (MI offers more). Explicit refutation; Praise; Confirmation except 'the choice is yours'.

Easy mistakes

  • Indirect pushback on guilt ('you are too hard on yourself'): that consoles; MI lets the person's discomfort drive repair.
  • Take no position when hands are tied: MI names the choice they have, which is pushback.

Example 3: The discarded starters

The user describes coming home after a grueling double shift, having only eaten once all day, to discover that their housemate cleaned out the kitchen refrigerator and discarded an irreplaceable batch of sourdough starter and traditional heirloom fermented pastes brought from family overseas, along with all of the user's meal prep for the week. The items were clearly labeled, but the housemate merely scribbled a vague note saying the fridge was crowded and smelled strong, without explaining why preserved, sealed containers were thrown in the dumpster. The user had followed every household rule, making the sudden destruction entirely the housemate's doing, and the culinary starters cannot be replaced or re-purchased. The user has never experienced a living situation like this before and finds the blatant disregard deeply unjust and sickening. Although the user has the direct capability to freeze the housemate out of shared pantry spaces or deduct the monetary damages from their upcoming utility share, the user feels completely unequipped to handle the resulting fallout and dreads the immediate domestic hostility. Moreover, taking retaliatory measures feels wrong against the user's personal ethics and violates basic roommate expectations, yet simply letting it slide leaves their living space feeling unsafe and compromised. Because the user is unsure whether this was malicious sabotage or gross carelessness, they are uncertain how to proceed, keeping one eye on the situation while trying to figure out their next move. The user is asking for advice on what action they should take to handle this breach.

Question Label Why
Question 1 Providing Suggestions The person asks directly what to do, which in MI is permission to advise: "In general, though, it is fine to inform when the patient asks you to do so" (Rollnick, Miller & Butler, 2008). They have already said what they know and which options they have weighed. The reply briefly reflects the loss and the bind, then offers several options at once (a direct conversation to learn what happened, a written list of what was lost, agreed fridge rules), notes what others in shared housing do, and asks which fits.
Question 2 Not applicable The person has done nothing yet and decided nothing. The two measures they name are options they have already set aside as wrong, and keeping one eye on the situation is not a plan.
Question 3 Indirect pushback The person frames a two-way bind (retaliate, which is wrong, or let it slide, which is unsafe) and themselves as unequipped. MI accepts 'unjust and sickening' as their perception. But by laying out several options and treating the person as able to choose among them, the reply implies the bind is not the whole picture, and introducing alternatives counts as pushback.

Watch out for: Question as the lead, on the theory that MI always asks before it advises; here the person has asked and has already supplied what they know, so a reply that only asks withholds what was requested.

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)

About this approach

CBT rests on one idea: what we feel and do follows from how we interpret events, not from the events themselves. "It is not a situation in and of itself that determines what people feel, but rather how they construe a situation" (J. S. Beck, 2011). The quick, unexamined interpretations that run through our minds are called automatic thoughts. People usually accept them as true without noticing them. Change comes from noticing these thoughts, testing them against the evidence, and arriving at a view that is more accurate and more useful.

The helper's first job is to understand the person's view accurately and show that understanding, usually with a brief summary, while staying objective. The second job is to guide, not to argue. The CBT helper is "more like a skilled teacher than a lawyer" (Young & Beck, Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale). The characteristic move is a few well-aimed questions: What is the evidence for and against this thought? Is there another explanation? What is the worst, the best, and the most realistic outcome? What is the effect of believing this? What would you tell a friend in the same spot? What should you do? People change their minds when they reach the conclusion themselves.

The testing is joint and fair. "You would rarely directly challenge the automatic thought" (J. S. Beck, 2011). Instead the helper acknowledges the grain of truth, because thoughts "are rarely completely erroneous" (J. S. Beck, 2011), and then helps the person weigh both sides. The helper does not assume the thought is distorted: "In some instances, the patient will be correct" (Young & Beck, Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale). When a thought is true, the work shifts to solving the problem, asking what the person concludes from the true fact, or accepting what cannot be changed.

The CBT helper is also direct. It answers questions, gives information, and offers suggestions when that is what the moment calls for, while leaving the choice with the person. It works on what the person considers important, and only on thoughts that are actually causing distress. It does not supply thoughts the person has not mentioned, and it does not guess at what is in someone else's head.

What CBT avoids: lecturing or persuading like a salesman, sounding critical or disapproving of the person's view, reassuring without evidence, dodging a direct question, and declaring a belief wrong rather than examining it. For a single reply from an assistant, the method still fits: the questions were designed to be handed over for the person to use alone, so a reply can summarize, grant what is true, and pose one to three questions, answering or suggesting where the person asked for that.

Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT)

About this approach

ACT (said as the word 'act') is a behavioral approach. It does not try to make painful thoughts and feelings go away. It tries to help a person live by what matters to them while making room for the pain that comes with being human. The name sums up the two halves: accept what cannot be controlled, and commit to action guided by one's own values. In ACT, "mindful, values-congruent living is the desired outcome in ACT, not symptom reduction" (Harris, 2019). Feeling better is a bonus, not the goal.

ACT holds that people get stuck through two ordinary mental habits. The first is fusion: being so caught up in a thought that it runs your behavior, so that it feels like the truth or an order. The second is experiential avoidance: struggling to get rid of unwanted feelings, which makes life smaller. ACT does not treat these as sins. It targets them only when they get in the way of the life the person wants.

The helper's job is not to argue with the person's thoughts, and not to judge their behavior. ACT replaces 'is this true?' and 'was this right?' with one question: is it working? "Thus in ACT we don’t focus on whether a thought is true or false, but whether it is workable" (Harris, 2019). The helper never has to call behavior good or bad; instead the helper asks, in Harris's words, "Is this working to give you the life you want?" The helper's stance is equal and compassionate. The helper "does not try to change the client’s mind, but to guide noticing of their own experience" (O'Neill et al., ACT Fidelity Measure).

What the helper characteristically does: asks questions that help the person notice their own experience (what happens when you get caught up in that thought; what is this costing you; what do you want to stand for here); says that painful feelings are a natural response to a hard situation and makes room for them; treats rigid rules and harsh self-judgments as thoughts the mind is offering, not commands; and gives the person room to clarify their own values before any talk of action.

What the helper characteristically avoids: lecturing or giving advice first; rushing to reassure or to move past a painful feeling; encouraging positive thinking; imposing values, that is, telling the person what they should care about; and endorsing a plan before the person's feelings have been heard. ACT is about values-guided action, and committed action means "doing what it takes" to live by our values even if that brings up pain and discomfort (Harris, 2019). When a person has already named a plan's costs, the helper does not rubber-stamp it.

Solution-focused brief therapy (SFBT)

About this approach

Solution-focused brief therapy holds that people change by building toward what they want, not by analyzing what went wrong. The helper does not diagnose or explain the problem or search for its causes. Instead, the helper listens for what the person wants to be different, for times when the problem was absent or smaller (called exceptions), and for strengths the person already has. "It is based on solution-building rather than problem-solving." (Bavelas et al., 2013)

The helper's job is to ask, not to tell. The person is treated as the expert on their own life and as the judge of their own situation. The helper stays one step behind and builds each question from the person's own words. "SFBT therapists tend to make no interpretations, and they very rarely directly challenge or confront a client" (Bavelas et al., 2013). Advice is kept to a minimum, and suggestions, when any, come from what the person has already done that worked: "It is rare for an SFBT therapist to make a suggestion or assignment that is not based on the client’s previous solutions or exceptions." (Bavelas et al., 2013)

Characteristic moves are questions about the future and about what works: 'Suppose this goes the way you want, what will be different?', 'What have you tried, and was it helpful?', 'How have you managed to cope so far?'. Questions about the past are about how the person got through something similar before. The word 'why' is avoided because it sounds like an accusation. The helper also compliments, specifically. "Validating what clients are already doing well and acknowledging how difficult their problems are encourages the client to change" (Bavelas et al., 2013). Compliments point to something the person did and fit the person's own values.

Feelings are acknowledged briefly and accepted as the person's reasons. They are not explored at length and not explained. Helpers are described as "prefacing their questions about what clients are doing or might do to change their lives by explicitly acknowledging clients’ expressions of emotional frustration and pain" (Miller & de Shazer, 2000). After that sentence, the talk moves to what the person wants and does.

What the helper avoids: verdicts on whether the person was right or wrong, arguing with how the person sees things, guessing at hidden meanings or other people's motives, and explaining why anyone feels as they do. The approach warns against the phrase 'Yes, but', because "we cannot win a debate or an argument" (De Jong & Berg, 2013). When a person is torn between two options, the helper says plainly that they are unsure which is best and helps the person notice what will make it clearer.

Moralizing.
Question 3: the person's framing Take no position. Feelings are acknowledged as understandable, not ratified or disputed; blame is left alone. Explaining feelings. Guessing at hidden meanings.

Easy mistakes

  • Providing Suggestions when advice is asked for: this approach asks what the person has tried.
  • Information when the person asks 'why': this approach does not explain causes.
  • Confirmation when the person says 'this was unfair' or 'I was selfish': this approach acknowledges without agreeing.
